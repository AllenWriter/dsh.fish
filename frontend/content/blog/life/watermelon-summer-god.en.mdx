Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. This summer is too hot. This episode we skip miracle drugs and talk about the most ordinary watermelon. The giant fruit everyone loves in summer. You know it. Summer is watermelon time, right? Heat off, thirst off, a slice — nothing more fitting.

So we should know it properly. Origin, history, a lot of fun. As a fruit it is also very unlike others. After you fully know watermelon, eating it may feel different. Try it.

Biology: eudicot, Cucurbitales, Cucurbitaceae, genus Citrullus, same family as the bottle gourd.

Several common melons are this family: winter melon, watermelon, pumpkin, cucumber, luffa, muskmelon — all gourd family.

Someone may wonder: where do the names come from? Why 西瓜, west melon? Pretty casual, same trick as 南瓜, south melon. Neither is native to China. Pumpkin came from the south, so south melon. Watermelon came from Central Asia, the west, so west melon. That's it.

English is watermelon, literally water melon, also apt. Water content is especially high. About 92% water. Compared with other fruit, the juice is obviously more. Eat watermelon and it will drip. Everyone knows. That's why it kills thirst. Hence watermelon.

Japanese is the most fun: スイカ. The word came from Chinese, a sound-loan of 西瓜, imitating some southern Chinese dialects. The coincidence: in Japanese that sound can also be read as "water melon," like a translation of watermelon. A pun that stuck.

The water-heavy trait is tied to origin. Next —

Watermelon's home is Africa. Many wild Citrullus grow in dry regions, sandy soil. Some look a lot like modern watermelons, just small, one-hand size. Color and stripes, clearly watermelon.

Cut them open: another story. The flesh is not red, basically white or pale green, some hard, some sponge-soft, taste bitter and astringent. Nothing you would eat as-is.

So it's odd, right? Why would anyone start planting this. Hard to see.

A similar question came up in the corn episode. Corn's ancestor also looked inedible, hard as pebbles. Scientists guess fire popped it into popcorn, Native Americans saw the value.

Wild watermelon's hidden value is the water.

The Kalahari in southwest Africa has a wild melon, tsamma. In the dry season locals use it as a water source, little kettles, easy to tap. Later besides wild ones they planted some as a reserve. That was huge for surviving drought.

The water-storage is also the plant's survival strategy. In the rains, drink fast, store in the flesh. In the dry, that water attracts animals. Animals eat it and spread seed. That's the point.

In dry country the trait is very useful. Humans and animals both like watermelon. Humans likely started planting and breeding it for the water. That's the guess.

Genus Citrullus has seven living species. Which is the wild ancestor of the modern fruit? Origin starts with Egyptian tombs more than 4,000 years ago.

When did humans start planting? Clear records: ancient Egypt. At least 4,000-plus years of domestication. Tomb paintings already look like today's, elongated, not the usual round wild fruit, so likely cultivated.

Next: which wild species did Egyptians tame? That puzzled scientists a long time. Several wild kinds could fit.

West Africa also grows a wild one, the egusi / colocynth type. Locals don't use it as water or fruit. They eat the seeds — many, nutritious, fat and protein. They make egusi soup, a traditional dish. People once guessed this might be the ancestor. Only a guess.

In 2019 scientists finally found a clue.

In Egyptian belief, death is not the end. You go to the sky. Tombs get travel necessities: bread, honey, meat, clothes, money, even pets, as grave goods.

What about drinking water? Yes: watermelon.

Egyptians put watermelon in tombs for the dead to drink on the road. Pharaohs too. Tutankhamun's tomb had watermelon seeds and paintings. Already very common.

The clue sat in a 3,500-year-old tomb. When it was excavated, fragments of watermelon leaf were found on a mummy. Archaeologists saved them.

In 2019 a German team studied the fragments. Preservation was so good they could still pull high-quality DNA. Genetic analysis put this watermelon close to a Sudanese subspecies, Kordofan melon.

Sudan has not been peaceful, always fighting, so finding the wild ancestor has to wait.

From the fragments and other finds, scientists also saw two important mutations.

One: the gene that makes cucurbitacin mutated. Why are wild watermelons bitter? Cucurbitacin. The mutation means by then they were already not bitter.

Another: the gene that makes lycopene. Lycopene is why tomatoes are red, and why watermelon is red. That mutation means the flesh was already red.

Research also finds the sweetness gene is bound to the lycopene gene. Meaning 3,500 years ago, Egyptian watermelon may already have tasted good, not only a water source.

Tasting good also created the condition for spreading worldwide. If it only stored water, other places might not need it. The watermelon we eat now owes a lot to the Egyptian foundation.

From Egypt, thousands of years of breeding worldwide, now over a thousand varieties. Flesh not always red, shape not always round. All kinds.

As the giant-fruit mascot, of course many people get obsessed with growing it bigger and bigger.

The current Guinness largest: 2013, a farmer in Tennessee, **159 kg**. Ridiculous. No idea how they ate it.

Big, and expensive. Some varieties are treated as high-end. Japan has a kind called Densuke watermelon, black-skin, said to be extremely good. Before sale it has to be ground and polished. That particular.

How expensive? In 2008 one Densuke auctioned for **650,000 yen**. Eye-opening.

Some people eating watermelon hate the seeds. No seeds would be better. So seedless appeared.

Seedless was first invented by Japanese, about 1939. Method: an alkaloid, colchicine, treat ordinary diploid seed or seedling, make it tetraploid, then cross tetraploid with diploid, get triploid. Triploid cannot form seed, so seedless. That's the principle.

Watermelon is only an ordinary fruit, and some people even built discrimination on it.

The cause is the U.S. Civil War, mentioned in the last cotton episode. After slavery was abolished, many African Americans left cotton plantations, started growing and selling watermelon to live.

The planters could not enslave them, felt bad, right. So they made a public opinion to mock: they are lazy, a piece of watermelon and they are satisfied. A very arrogant pose.

Later some people used eating watermelon to discriminate against Black people, to show superiority. Even now some still bring it up. Ridiculous.

A rotten meme from a hundred-plus years ago should have been retired long ago. That's this Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. Thanks for listening. See you next time.
