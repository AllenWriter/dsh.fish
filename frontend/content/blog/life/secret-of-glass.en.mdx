Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. I'm your host, Qiao Yulan.

Today: something that's everywhere, that you see walking and driving every day. Glass.

Bright, clear, all kinds of pretty colors. Who would think a simple pane also left an unerasable mark on the history of science? Because of glass, Newton, Galileo and the rest could watch physics through lenses and measure with test tubes.

It also pushed how we know optics, until lithography machines could carve a chip on a fingernail, the smart life that's now everywhere.

This episode: the secret of glass, again.

Its origin goes back thousands of years B.C. — earlier than Jesus. Records put the earliest glass in Egypt five or six thousand years ago. Ceramic workers heating clay accidentally found bright shards, then after experiment after experiment fired sand and soda into vessels.

The main chemistry is silicon dioxide. Yes, the same as sand and semiconductor chips. Sand melts at 1700°C. For glass you add soda to drop the melting point, lime, fire, cool, and you get the shine.

The Sword of Goujian, unearthed in China, has glass set in the hilt. We didn't call it glass then. Liuli. In the idiom 光怪陆离, 陆离 is glass. The phrase means strange shapes, riot of color.

We met glass that early — so why are most old Chinese buildings paper windows? In TV, an assassin slits a gap and sees everyone inside. Ancient Chinese buildings are wood. A room can't take the weight of that much glass. Plus heat. No AC. Paper windows feel better than glass, more practical. So when we hear "glass," the first thought is maybe it's modern.

Ancient European buildings are mostly stone. Glass is less of a load. Medieval cathedrals still standing in Europe use huge glass windows, even roofs. Pictures on the glass, all colors.

History says hello here. On this simple object, East and West fork.

An obvious split is painting. Some of you, like me, look at Eastern ink and feel art: a few strokes, a mountain, a sea. Early Western painting is similar. God gets a halo, not much detail, perspective is so-so.

After the Renaissance, Western realism jumps in a way that feels illegal. Mona Lisa, Girl with a Pearl Earring — like a physics-engine 3D render. My first thought: da Vinci is almost a human camera. Not just him. Some paintings even have background blur. That's not an eye. That's a camera. Did they cheat?

Yes. Better to call those masters engineers who could run optical kit and human anatomy. A lot of evidence: when they painted portraits they sat in another small room and, through glass, printed the sitter onto the canvas. They copied the projection. Living portraits.

From about the 15th to 19th century, Western painters commonly used camera lucidas, camera obscuras, concave mirrors.

That's a watershed. An unremarkable little thing, glass, becomes the tool for knowing optics, another angle on the world. When you can look with tools, churches and authority have a harder time fooling you.

Someone counted: of the twenty greatest experiments in history, sixteen used glass instruments. Newton's mechanics, the foundation. How do you calculate gravity between bodies? An apple doesn't write the paper. Hypothesis, then test. To test the sky you need a glass telescope.

Chemistry and biology too: transparent vessels to watch results. The steam engine that lights the first industrial revolution is, at root, the motion of pressure. You need a glass barometer. Age of sail, no GPS: a sextant to find yourself, and a bit of glass on it.

Glass, frankly, is deconstructing the world. Things you couldn't see, put in front of the eye. Want a centimeter? Make a ruler that can measure a millimeter. No independent tool, no correct practice.

From then on, knowing the world is not the heart. It's the eye. Watch stars, watch bacteria, examine everything. Humanity suddenly has an instrument of interrogation for nature. That instrument is glass.

When people have a tool to see natural phenomena themselves, and it isn't a royal monopoly, and it isn't that expensive — ordinary merchants can buy it — the seed of fact-following science is planted, step by step.

After the Renaissance, the Enlightenment. Little glass carries Western civilization onto the tech trees of chemistry, astronomy, physics. Among those leaves the brightest is modern semiconductor chips. Lithography is the key ring. No mature scanner, no cheap smart chips, no compute peak, no AI.

In lithography, glass is pushed to a new peak. Those glass lenses are 40% of a scanner's cost. Simply: a lithography machine is a giant camera for making chips. We photograph to lock the world in a picture. The scanner exposes a circuit drawing onto resist-coated silicon. A good camera, high-res photos. A serious scanner, a precise chip.

Camera resolution we know: two hundred million pixels. On a scanner, resolution is process: 90 nm, 45 nm. Smaller nanometers, more compute. Like a city on a fingernail: more towers, more compute units, stronger chip.

That's why Zeiss, famous for lenses, is also a supplier to ASML, the Dutch lithography leader. Master glass lenses and you master a scientific view of optics, a discipline.

A small piece of glass, running through human history like this. Smartphones, computers, the NIO you're driving with assisted driving. From the micro world to the edge of the cosmos, glass keeps stretching the field of view. More important: it pushes us to stop knowing the world with the heart, and to observe, understand reality, use scientific thinking to know and change it.
