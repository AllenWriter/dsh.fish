Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. The person we talk about today,

if he lived now, would be academic top-stream — a name you cannot skip in school. Mechanics in physics, calculus in math, gravity in astronomy, start with him. Isaac Newton, called by many the greatest scientist in human history. What you may not know: this science god's life is much richer than the textbook. World-changing discoveries before 26, after 30 addicted to stocks as a "leek," academic palace fights with the big names of his time. Today we put down perfect textbook Newton and talk about a life that is great and a little stabbing in its truth.

(SFX: a light page turn)

First, the cheat-code start. 1665, London, a great plague — yes, the famous one, more than 80,000 dead, the city terrified. Twenty-two-year-old Newton was at Trinity, Cambridge. The school sent students home. Today a lot of people would squat at home, scroll, order takeout. Newton? He locked himself in the country attic and entered closed-door cultivation.

Guess what he did in those 18 months? Put it this way: those months match another person's lifetime of research. First the generalized binomial theorem — later a basic tool of higher math. Then worse, he started grinding calculus (yes, the course that now gives undergrads a headache). Not named yet, but he had the core. More crucial: two "final questions." Why does the apple fall (later gravity). What is light (later optics). People later called these 18 months Newton's "year of wonders," like a wuxia hero falling into a cave and finding a secret book, meridians opened overnight — except Newton's secret book was his own brain.

(SFX: a bell, back to campus)

Plague over, Newton back to school, then took his teacher Barrow's chair as Lucasian Professor of Mathematics. Not a small post: Cambridge's academic throne. Holders are big names — Hawking, who wrote A Brief History of Time, held it 30 years. By rights such a person would pack the hall. Newton would not play by the book. Lectures too hardcore, students did not follow, fewer came, finally almost nobody signed up. You think that hurt him? Opposite. Nobody to bother him, more focus: he built the first reflecting telescope (much clearer than the refractors of the day), organized optics, even had tea with the Queen — academic top-stream, properly.

(SFX: tense violin)

Good days did not last. Newton met the enemy of his life: Robert Hooke. Not a small role, a few years older, already a Royal Society big: Hooke's law (spring force proportional to deformation), microscope master, even named the word cell (yes, the cell in biology class, first shouted by Hooke staring at cork). If they had cooperated, maybe more big finds. One had a temper, one was a contrarian. Together: Mars hitting Earth.

First clash: optics. Newton said light is particles (later corpuscular). Hooke backed waves (Huygens's camp later). Newton wrote a paper to rebut, first paragraph named and cursed Hooke. Hooke jumped: who are you to say I'm wrong? I am the microscope's ancestor. Worse: Newton's Optics, held for years, could have come out earlier. Hooke and Huygens ganged up, Newton locked the manuscript in a drawer in a fit, published after Hooke died — Huygens gone too, nobody to argue, corpuscular theory held the screen for a hundred-plus years, until 1905 Einstein showed wave-particle duality (neither Newton nor Hooke was all wrong).

(SFX: low narrative music)

The classic clash is still gravity. We love the apple-hit-Newton story. Honestly, probably invented. Newton's niece, to puff uncle, said an apple hit his head. Voltaire added oil and wrote it into a book. The world believed. Newton studied gravity because he had read Hooke and others on planetary motion, especially Hooke's inverse-square guess (gravity falls with distance squared). Hooke's math was weak, only a rough guess.

Newton was different. He pulled out calculus, nine pages: if gravity is inverse square, orbits must be ellipses (later proven right). He wrote Principia (the bible of science). Publication needed his friend Halley's money (the comet Halley later found). Hooke would not have it: I said inverse square first. You used my idea. He asked Halley to beg: at least mention my name. Newton grudgingly footnoted "Hooke discussed similar problems." Hooke thought the weight too light, kept nagging. Newton went dark — second edition, every mention of Hooke deleted. Hooke died gloomy. After Newton became Royal Society president, when the Society moved, he destroyed Hooke's manuscripts and portrait. We do not even know what Hooke looked like. Some guess a severe hunch. Newton's "if I have seen further it is by standing on the shoulders of giants" (written to Hooke) may have been shade: with that hunch, you cannot even reach my shoulder.

(SFX: a sarcastic little laugh)

Newton's fight skill was not only Hooke. He also fought Flamsteed, Astronomer Royal at Greenwich — forty years, 2,935 stars, records absurdly precise, too much a perfectionist, delayed publishing the catalog. In 1680 two comets appeared in Europe. Flamsteed judged they were one comet going and returning. Newton first opposed, later changed his mouth and never mentioned the Astronomer Royal's contribution. Worse: Newton's "good teammate" Halley (yes, that Halley) secretly gave Newton the unpublished catalog. Newton used it. In 1712, as Society president, he and Halley published it. Flamsteed spent his own money buying back 400 copies, burned 300 on the spot. Six years after he died, the widow printed the real catalog.

(SFX: tense standoff music)

Then the calculus war with German genius Leibniz. We now know they invented calculus independently (Newton fluxions, Leibniz cleaner symbols). At the time Newton, to grab "inventor," had the Society issue an anonymous report saying Newton's method was better — later found the "anonymous reviewer" was Newton himself. Leibniz jumped. Continental scientists lined up with Leibniz, British scholars stuck to Newton's symbols, British math fell a hundred-plus years behind, the academic center drifted to France.

(SFX: softer music)

We should not only stare at the black history. Any one of his achievements before 26 is epoch-making: calculus as a base of modern math, gravity explaining the heavens, optics showing light, the reflecting telescope still in observatories. In 1696 Swiss mathematician Johann Bernoulli, to mess with all Europe's mathematicians, set the brachistochrone problem and secretly mailed it to Newton to slap his face. Newton got home at 4 p.m., pulled an all-nighter, mailed the answer at 4 a.m. — signed "an old Englishman" (Newton was 54). Bernoulli received it and froze: from the claw I know the lion. Meaning: this style of solving, it is Newton.

(SFX: warm ending music)

So what was the real Newton? A genius, at the peak of science before 26. A mortal, who held grudges, envied, tore faces with peers for fame, even late in life addicted to alchemy (turn metal to gold), and lost his shirt in stocks (said to have lost even the underwear). That complexity makes him more real. Science never advanced on perfect saints. It advanced on people who dared think, dared fight, even a bit of a thorn.

Next time you hear the apple story, you can laugh and tell a friend: don't believe it, maybe the niece made it up, maybe Voltaire added a scene. Don't forget: what made Newton great was not the apple, not Hooke, not any fight — those 18 months in the plague, every page of formula in a lonely attic, the eternal asking of why about this world.
