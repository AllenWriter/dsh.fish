Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box.

Today we open a box about **viruses and bacteria**. A fight on the edge of death, a medical miracle, and a true story about love that would not let go.

It starts as an ordinary trip, and pulls in humanity's last stand against superbugs.

Late 2015. Retired American Tom Patterson and his wife Steffanie have just seen the pyramids in Egypt when Patterson collapses. Abdominal pain, violent vomiting, and in three days **toxic shock**. A superbug has him.

Not a pharaoh's curse, but the tourist site is not innocent. Thousands of people from everywhere visit the pyramids every day. Bacteria pool there, then hitch a ride to every corner of the planet.

Patterson is rushed back to the U.S. He is so sick that American medicine has to reopen Pandora's box and fight the most dangerous enemy with the oldest method.

Then, on Patterson's body, a species war to the death. This case might change how humans treat superbugs.

End of October 2015, Patterson is taken to UC San Diego School of Medicine. He and Steffanie are both medical people at that university, so he gets the best care they have.

By then he is not lucid. The bacterium inside him is one of the toughest on earth: **Acinetobacter baumannii**, resistant to almost every human antibiotic. A superbug.

Watching the husband of many years walk toward death, Steffanie is frantic. She talks treatment with the doctors every day.

One day, on a conference call, the doctors think she has hung up and start discussing "after." Someone says: someone should tell Steffanie he's almost gone. She should face it.

She hears it like lightning. She breaks, shouts into the phone: "No. I will save him. I will not give up."

Bacteria are among Earth's oldest lives, about 3.7 billion years. Prokaryotes, no nucleus, simple. Animals, plants, fungi — including us — are eukaryotes, nucleus, more complex.

There are about 1.5 × 10³⁰ bacteria, more than all animal and plant cells combined. They're everywhere: air, water, food. About 80% are harmless or even helpful. Some cause tetanus, typhoid, syphilis, food poisoning, cholera.

The fight against bacteria only turns in 1929. Fleming, studying staph, accidentally finds that Penicillium can kill bacteria. Humanity's first **antibiotic, penicillin**. Broad-spectrum drugs follow.

Bacteria do not surrender. Fast breeding, high-frequency mutation, they evolve resistance. Ninety years later, superbugs have us empty-handed again.

That's Steffanie's bind: Patterson has no drug left.

She thinks it through and decides. At the bed she takes his hand: "Do you still want to live? I have one last method. It might save you, it might not. I need your permission. Will you let me try something unconventional? If yes, squeeze my hand."

Nothing at first. A few seconds later, the hand tightens. She checks again. He squeezes hard.

She decides to use something very old she learned years ago, with huge potential: **phage therapy**.

Bacteria are prokaryotes, humans eukaryotes. Earth also has something stranger: viruses. They may be older than bacteria. In 1899 the Dutch microbiologist Beijerinck first names the virus.

Early 20th century, a British scientist, Twort, finds a virus that infects bacteria — the **bacteriophage**. Modern virology starts there.

A phage looks like a tiny robot: icosahedral head, DNA or RNA inside, a hollow tail spike that injects the genetic payload into the bacterium and kills it.

Earth's phage count is estimated at 10³¹, an order of magnitude more than bacteria.

Whether a virus is life is still argued. Most scientists say it doesn't have the features of life: it can't reproduce itself, no metabolism, it only wants to copy itself.

Steffanie moves. She contacts the Naval Medical Research Center in Maryland and Texas A&M, asking for phages against A. baumannii. She also races the FDA for an experimental treatment that has never been done.

She signs the life-and-death papers. All risk on her.

Soon the FDA approves. Experts worldwide — Switzerland, Belgium, Poland, Croatia, India — send usable phage samples.

The experiment starts at the San Diego medical school.

The phage sticks to the bacterium with its tail, injects DNA. The infected bacterium is forced to make a swarm of new phages, then lyses. New phages spill out and hunt the next target.

A virus hunting bacteria.

Forty-eight hours after injection, Patterson slowly opens his eyes. Vitals come back. The superbug in him drops hard.

He walks back from the gate.

He survives because he has a wife who is calm, brave, and loves him. Later she asks if he heard her calling while he was under. He says he heard, didn't understand the words, only knew: **hold her hand**.

The miracle is the phage. Does that mean phages are ready for medicine? No.

Bacteria attack humans. Phages, as viruses, can attack humans and bacteria.

A virus's goal is simple: **copy its genes, generation after generation.**

Studying viruses helps medicine, and might even unlock why life exists, why the universe has life at all.

Patterson's case becomes North America's first successful phage treatment of a superbug. It will push the field.

Problems come with it. A phage is still a virus. Will it swap genes with us? Unknown consequences later? Nobody knows.

Maybe to fight one demon we let out another.

Blessing or curse? Or was it already written in the genes?
