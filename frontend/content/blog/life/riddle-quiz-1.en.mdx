We'll warm up with the most common object of all: the QR code. How much do you actually know?

QR codes were invented in Japan in 1994, and around 2011, riding the mobile-internet wave, they swept China. They're called two-dimensional because they're a square: a horizontal line and a vertical line making a space for information. So was the ancestor called a "one-dimensional horse"?

Yep. A 1D code is a single line — a barcode, the one the cashier scans at checkout.

In the age of barcodes people complained there wasn't enough room. Twenty-five letters or digits, that's it. In an internet-app explosion, that's obviously not enough.

A QR code stores information on both axes, so the density is higher. It can hold a few thousand characters now, plus redundancy, so even if part of the picture is damaged, the scan can still pop.

And have you noticed those fat little squares in the corners? Those are finder patterns. They tell the phone where to start reading. So whether you scan it upright, upside down, or at a tilt, the phone still gets the content.

The QR code is a door into the internet. A long, ugly string of characters that only a machine loves, packed into a tidy square (a bit of trypophobia, maybe). Nobody has to type the URL letter by letter. Adding a friend can be that elegant: scan, and it just appears. That's the QR code's origin story. You get it?

Guangdong car owners hear "shanghuo" — getting heaty — all the time. Lychees, oranges, fried chicken, barbecue: eat them and you rush for herbal tea to press the fire down. Skip that and you break out, cough, dry throat, right on cue.

Barbecue, sure, it came off a grill. But lychees and oranges? That glassy, juicy flesh — how did that get mixed up with fire?

To be clear, "heatiness" is a traditional Chinese-medicine idea. In modern-medicine language, it's basically inflammation.

The sore, dry throat after a lychee binge comes from dumping too much sugar into the body in a short window. Blood sugar spikes, insulin spikes, the pancreas clocks overtime. Oxidizing that sugar makes more free radicals, which nick cell membranes, which starts a little inflammatory response. The throat is a garrison for immune cells. White cells wrap the odd cells and head home to the throat, and you start coughing.

Why can such small fruit pack that punch? Because you eat too many. Lychees and oranges are tiny; once you start, you don't stop. And they're loaded with sugar: 100 g of lychee is 15–20 g of sugar, 100 g of orange is 8–10 g. The so-called sugar bomb, watermelon, is only about 6 g per 100 g of flesh, and it's mostly water, so you fill up. Watermelon doesn't feel as "hot."

Barbecue is the same logic, just fat. If sugar and fat climb too fast, the pancreas takes a hit, hormones wobble for a bit, the gut flora shifts, and you get pimples and constipation. In an age of plenty, a little less of that sweet burden also means a little less overtime for the cells doing the work.

Winter arrives, and a lot of people suddenly itch — arm, then thigh. No pimples, so why does winter skin itch? Cold air is dry, water evaporates off the surface faster. Temperature drops, the skin makes less oil, so it holds less water. Dry skin itches. It's a signal.

That's why hand cream and body lotion sell in winter. They lay down an oil film and lock the water in. Too lazy to put any on? Another option is to shower less in winter — which I know Guangdong people will never accept. So another decent trick: skip the body wash, or skip the soap, and leave a little of your own oil on. Less dry.
