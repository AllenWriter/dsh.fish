Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. Today the story of penicillin.

Before we start, a short note on the P-person in the title.

The word comes from MBTI, Perceiving. A clear trait: they keep open and flexible, dislike over-planning and constraint, go with the flow, adapt hard to accident and change.

Simply: P-people do not schedule life too tight. They "walk a step, see a step," and they can notice and meet sudden cases.

Did you know? A lot of great discoveries in history actually relate to this P trait. Today's lead, Alexander Fleming, is a typical P. A scientist, but not strict at all, even a bit of a nasty sense of humor.

That open, flexible, accident-sensitive character is exactly why we have penicillin, which saved countless lives. This miracle drug deeply shaped human history.

## Intro: a twentieth-century miracle drug

The change penicillin brought is not less than computers and the internet. It earned the name. On the battlefield it saved countless soldiers.

After the industrial revolution, war changed hugely in weapons. Cold steel to hot steel, wounds bigger and deeper, microbes a better chance to breed. After World War I people found more soldiers died of infection after the war than died directly on the field.

Against that, scientists put energy and time on microbes. Not every scientist was as lucky as our host Alexander Fleming. Penicillin's birth is largely a product of luck — more precisely, a lucky find brought by a P trait.

## The cruelty of World War I and the threat of infection

1914, World War I. People were stunned by modern war. Soldiers were no longer sword for sword. Bullets, explosives. Hot weapons make worse wounds, rot makes infection easier.

Fleming was then an army doctor at a field hospital in France. He watched countless wounded, who had barely escaped the field, die of infection in the hospital.

## How we came to know microbes

In the twentieth century people already knew microbes well. As early as the seventeenth, the Dutch Leeuwenhoek saw countless microbes in a feather through a microscope he invented. Later, nineteenth-century Louis Pasteur — the pasteurization founder — went deeper. Humanity already knew the world lives with a huge number of microbes far beyond imagination.

They exist almost everywhere. If the body is hurt, skin broken, the physiological barrier is broken, microbes breed in the wound, serious infection.

Before the nineteenth century, what surgeons feared most was not a failed operation, but the patient dying of infection after the cut. In 1865 British surgeon Joseph Lister said surgery needs disinfection, with disinfectant. Patients under Lister's sterile surgery had infection rates drop a lot.

Disinfectant is only prevention. If the patient is already infected, disinfectant then is useless. In World War I Fleming treated wounds with it, results were not good, and disinfectant itself is toxic, it wrecks the body's own immune cells. Fleming saw: you must find a weapon against bacteria more effective than disinfectant.

## First luck: lysozyme

Fleming's luck was very good. He at once found the first magic substance, and it came from his own snot.

1921, the third year after the war, Fleming had a bad cold, still insisted on working in the lab. He was culturing a lot of staph on plates. Undisturbed, staph expands its turf. In theory the whole round plate should fill with it.

Solid medium is transparent. Medium with staph looks cloudy. Scientists can tell by cloudiness whether a region is occupied.

Fleming came to the lab with a heavy cold. Who knows what he was thinking. Very nasty humor: he took his own snot, dropped a drop on the plate. Two weeks later he was shocked: except where the snot was, the rest of the plate was full of bacteria.

What does that mean? Fleming thought: likely something in the snot kills bacteria. Scientists are scientists, sensitive to detail. He did many other body-fluid experiments, found tears, saliva, most fluids have this magic bactericide, other life too — egg white, fish eggs.

He named it lysozyme. Before he found it in egg white he madly asked colleagues for tears, which made that crowd suffer. Later in eggs he solved the source, and dived into research.

Seven years. Result disappointed everyone. Lysozyme's antibacterial activity is very low, no effect on many pathogens. Fleming had to drop it.

## Second luck: the accidental find of penicillin — a P-person's sharp eye

We said at the start Fleming's luck is very good. Lysozyme's find ended so-so, but it was born from a sudden idea, and that luck was a paving for a bigger luck next.

Summer 1928, Fleming went on holiday with family, weeks out, back to the London lab at the end of August. Here the P trait: P-people usually do not stick too tight to strict lab process. Before he left, many bottles were not cleaned to standard, even media full of bacteria just sitting there.

Whether Fleming meant it, or simply did not follow strict lab rules, before the holiday those plates sat unmanaged. If Fleming were a strict J (Judging), packing everything clean every day to standard, penicillin might never have been found.

The key is another P trait: sharp observation of accident. Cleaning those plates, past experience let him see a very important detail: transparent rings. We explained: transparent means no bacteria growing, cloudy means occupied. Clearly the stuff in the middle of those rings kills bacteria.

That is how Fleming found Penicillium. Penicillium is also a microbe. The staph he cultured is a bacterium. Penicillium killed Staphylococcus aureus.

## War among microbes

The specific reason is not hard to guess. Fleming's plates were not covered, and there was water, easy to mold.

Some Penicillium landed in them, reproduced, expanded numbers, and released a substance that suppresses bacterial growth: penicillin.

Because Penicillium can secrete penicillin, it can suppress other microbes, make richer resources and territory for itself. On the plate you can see: staph dare not go near Penicillium. They chickened out, left the space around it, hence the transparent rings.

This fight among microbes is antagonism, a result of long evolution.

## A find ignored

Fleming was delighted. He found penicillin in September 1928, published in June 1929, a paper that would later take a Nobel. Unexpectedly, for the next ten years nobody paid attention.

Time to 1938. An Oxford team spotted Fleming's work, decided to extract penicillin. 1941, a policeman accidentally fell into a flower bed, eyes and face cut by rose thorns, the wound became sepsis. Even today sepsis mortality is very high.

The Oxford team gave the extracted penicillin to this patient. At first the effect was very good. The patient still died, not because penicillin was wrong, because the lab amount was too small.

## Breakthrough in industrial production

Relying on a lab to make a drug is completely not enough. You need a modern pharma factory at scale. Unluckily Britain was already in World War II. The team could not find a plant to partner.

Still late 1941, Japan hit Pearl Harbor, America entered the war. The Oxford people finally found hope in the U.S. They persuaded the government, made more people see penicillin would be a weapon on the field, partnered with U.S. plants, finally broke the low-yield problem.

Then batch penicillin left the line, went to the field, became the Allies' most important weapon besides guns. Many wounded improved fast, back in weeks. Victory in WWII, penicillin's credit is huge.

## Fleming's honor

After, Fleming naturally became the focus of all eyes. Nobel, and in Michael Hart's book of 100 people who influenced human history, Fleming is 45th.

A few names around 40th so you feel it: 40th, Plato; 42nd, Beethoven; 43rd, Heisenberg, a founder of quantum mechanics. See how high Fleming sits.

## The arms race with bacteria

With antibiotics, humanity formally opened an arms race with bacteria. Superbugs, I believe you know the word: bacteria resistant to antibiotics.

Antibiotics are a double-edged sword. Fleming maybe would not have thought: humans work so hard iterating antibiotics, still cannot catch bacterial evolution. Because of antibiotics, countless people escaped death. Also because of antibiotics, many die of superbug infection.

## Close: a P-person's lesson

Today's story tells an interesting point: sometimes it is exactly the flexibility, openness, and sensitivity to accident that P-people have that makes a great find. Of course I am not saying a strict scientific attitude does not matter. I am saying keep an open mind and a sharp eye, and sometimes the surprise sits in the most careless place.
