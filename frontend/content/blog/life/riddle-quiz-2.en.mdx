Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. I'm your host, Qiao Yulan. Have you ever run into some perfectly ordinary thing, or some idea everyone repeats, and then thought about it for two seconds and realized you have no idea why it exists? Like: why is the smell of winter barbecue so seductive, what chemical secret is hiding behind it. After a big meal the body feels heavy — how do you eat and drink in a way that is actually scientific?

Besides calories, how does insulin shape modern life?

How many people are like me: walking past a grill shop in the cold, the body still on the sidewalk, the soul already kidnapped by the smell. God, why is grilled meat that fragrant? Science's answer: barbecue, toast, that aroma, is the Maillard reaction happening as food heats.

When amino acids — a big part of meat and protein — mix with carbohydrates, the sugars and starches we know, and you heat them together, the color goes from raw-meat red toward deep brown, and a stack of complicated flavors and smells appears. A lot of chemistry happens in there: sugar reduction, amino acids deaminating and rearranging.

Maillard. Sounds like a person's name. Can you really publish a paper over a plate of barbecue? You really can.

The first person to notice this and put it in a journal was a French biochemist named Louis Camille Maillard. His day job was synthesizing proteins.

In 1912, in an experiment meant to mimic biochemistry inside the body, he heated amino acids with sugars, watched the mix darken, and noticed a distinctive smell. He published in 1913, then stopped. In a war-torn decade, having something to eat was already a win. Who had time to study how to make food smell better?

Not until 1953, after World War II, did chemist Hodge at a USDA lab fill in the mechanism — the actual equations — in Maillard's paper. Peace arrived. People no longer wanted only to fill the stomach. They would pay for better food at home.

That is when the food industry took off. Bosses desperately wanted the code for making food that would please most consumers, faster, at scale. A few chefs' family recipes in a kitchen were never going to do it.

The Maillard reaction sitting in a journal made industrial deliciousness possible. It is basically the password to the human palate. Anywhere you bake, grill, or heat — roasting coffee, baking bread, turning cacao into chocolate — using Maillard well is how you hit "just right."

Side note: Maillard colors are also a fall-winter fashion this year. Next to the usual black-white-gray coats, those deep reds, yellow-browns, caramel tones feel like food: warm.

Barbecue, cake, milk tea are great. Somewhere inside, a rational voice still yells: stop eating, one winter of this and you put on ten jin.

So let's dig. What actually makes the body gain fat? Come on, calories, right? Eat more, move less, you get fat. Yes, the calorie model is famous. Problem: its history goes back to nineteenth-century nutrition, around when conservation of energy was proposed.

The model's core is balance of intake and expenditure. Follow the formula, the body will not get fat, or will gain on command. Control diet and exercise, any ideal weight is yours. In real life we know short-term weight change is brutally hard, and some people eat and never gain. So what broke?

A 1944 starvation study in Minnesota found the answer: the calorie model is wrong. The body has its own energy-balance system. You cannot treat intake and burn as static numbers.

World War II was ending. Europe had hungry refugees. The U.S. government wanted to know the long-term effects of starvation, to plan postwar nutrition aid. There were no starving refugees on U.S. soil, so they funded the University of Minnesota for what now looks like a deeply inhumane experiment.

Thirty-six male volunteers, followed for a year. First three months: 3,200 calories a day — about eleven meat buns. Then six months of starvation at 1,570 — about five buns. Last three months, back to 2,000–3,200.

Scientists did the arithmetic. Subjects should lose 35.3 kg on average in the hunger phase. They only lost 16.8. Worse: almost everyone got colder, weaker, furious, depressed. In the three months of refeeding, many binged. Weight and body fat ended higher than before the experiment.

That should not happen. Variables were controlled. Conservation of energy should not allow this. Scientists kept going and found the model's own flaw: intake and expenditure are not independent. Everyone has a homeostatic system.

When intake drops, burn and metabolism drop too, until a new balance. If you suddenly break the diet, intake is much higher than before, burn is still low, weight shoots up like a rocket.

That is why weight loss feels so hard, and why, the moment you succeed, the kilos sprint back. The body, without a new overall balance, is desperate to claw the old one back.

Finding a hole in the old theory is only step one of a new one. In a 1993 insulin experiment, four groups of diabetic patients got different insulin doses. Scientists cut calories for one group. That group's weight did not fall. It rose with the insulin dose.

So is the key not calories, but insulin? There is evidence. Before synthetic insulin, people with type 1 diabetes had almost none. They could eat and not gain.

How does insulin get mixed up with weight? Look at how it works. When you eat, the small intestine absorbs nutrients and breaks them into glucose. The pancreas dumps insulin, a hormone that helps cells take glucose out of the blood.

Think of energy storage as a small fridge you open often and a big freezer. Insulin parks energy in the small fridge as glycogen. When that fridge is full, leftover energy goes into the freezer as fat.

Naturally, after you stop eating for a while, the body burns glycogen from the small fridge first. Empty that, then pull fat from the freezer. Healthy. In theory burning fat is more efficient, and everyone likes watching fat go — that is getting lean, right?

But if the pancreas keeps secreting insulin — cells become insulin-resistant, they need more insulin to move blood sugar — and the body gets used to high insulin, another story starts. Excess insulin is a signal: there is already plenty of blood sugar, we do not need to burn fat. It is blocking fat-burning. You cannot get energy, you feel hungry, you eat again. More insulin. Fat stays stuck.

That is the newer nutrition model: the carbohydrate-insulin model, CIM.

Based on that, not gaining in winter is simple in principle: let the pancreas rest. Slow the insulin. Don't put it on 996. Stretch the gaps between meals. Eat fewer high-carb staples — rice, noodles.

These two theories make one thing clear: the body is a wildly complex system. Everyone is different. Blaming obesity on "eating" is too thin. Metabolism, hormones, genetics, gut microbes, plus other biological and environmental factors all play in intake, storage, and burn.

I like a line: every person is a little universe. The cells and microbes living in the body keep their own ecology.
