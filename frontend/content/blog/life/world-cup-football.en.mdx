Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. Did you watch the World Cup? These last few days, not long after kickoff, a very particular match: Iran vs New Zealand.

On the score it is a group game, 2–2. Put the background outside the pitch in, and it is not ordinary at all. One host of this World Cup is the United States, and Iran is playing on U.S. soil.

In an ordinary year that might only be visas, security, diplomatic protocol. Today it is very sensitive. Conflict, sanctions, talks, military pressure between the U.S. and Iran have tangled for decades. This year the relationship is stretched to the limit. Against that, Iranian players still took visas, walked into a Los Angeles stadium, and finished a match in front of the world's cameras.

That is the strangest thing about football.

It is only a game. Twenty-two people chase a ball, cheer if it goes in, sigh if it doesn't. Sometimes humans give it a very serious job: when politics cannot talk, diplomacy is stuck, war has blocked every sentence, football can still put enemies under the same set of rules for ninety minutes.

Today, using this World Cup news, a simple chat: where did football come from? Why is it always tangled with war, conflict, peace? How was the modern World Cup born? And why did a sport that claims to belong to all humanity become a huge business.

First, origins.

Trace history and you find something interesting: many places, with no contact, invented similar "ball games."

Ancient Greece had Episkyros, we can call it old E. Young men in two teams on sand, grabbing a leather ball, hands and feet allowed. Rome had a similar play, Harpastum, old H. More like army training: soldiers push, crash, grab in mud, fitness and fight.

Han China had cuju, popular in court and army. North American indigenous people had a lacrosse ancestor. Maya in Mesoamerica were more extreme: a ball game, no hands or feet, only hip and elbow toward a stone ring.

Look: these civilizations had no WeChat, no flights, no World Cup broadcast, and they all did the same thing: put a round thing on the ground or in the middle of a field, then contest it with the body.

What does that say? Ball sports were probably not one nation's sudden genius. They are a low-level human instinct. We like chase, we like contest, we like stuffing chaotic force into a set of rules. Football later swept the world for this: simple enough, primitive enough. No complex kit. A patch of empty ground, a ball, a few people, you start.

Because it is so primitive, the relationship with war has always been subtle.

The most famous story: Christmas 1914.

World War I had just started. On the Western Front, British and German soldiers lay in their trenches, no-man's-land in between. Wire, craters, bodies not yet collected.

On the night of the 24th, small candlelight went up on the German side. Someone sang Silent Night. British soldiers froze, then sang the English version. Next morning a few German soldiers came out without guns, hands up, clumsy English: Merry Christmas, we have no weapons.

Then an unbelievable scene. More and more British and German soldiers climbed out, walked into no-man's-land. Handshake, cigarettes, chocolate, buttons, photos of family. Some buried dead comrades together. Later someone produced a football, and they played a makeshift match among the craters.

You cannot say how official that game was. Scores differ. The thing itself is enough: people who had been shooting each other days before, because of Christmas, because of a ball, briefly saw the other as a person again.

Of course war does not stop for one match. High command soon forbade private contact. Later Christmases never saw a ceasefire that large. But that ball rolling in no-man's-land in 1914 stayed in memory. It proved: inside an extremely cruel war machine, humanity had not fully vanished.

Second story: Kiev, 1942.

Nazi Germany occupied Kiev. Many Ukrainian players were dead or POWs. Some, to live, did hard labor at a local bakery. Later, to paint "normal life" in the occupied zone, the Germans had these bakery workers form a team to play German army sides.

The team was FC Start. Call it Start.

Germans did not take them seriously. Start beat the Luftwaffe side 5–1. Nazi officers felt humiliated, arranged a rematch. Before it, everyone knew: not an ordinary match. If Start won again, consequences could be severe.

They still won, 5–3.

Later some Start players were arrested by Gestapo. Some died in camps. There are films, and disputes over details — whether they were executed right after winning. The core fact does not change: in an occupied city, people without guns used football as a public resistance.

1914, football let enemies briefly embrace. 1942, football let the occupied keep dignity. Football's special place is not that it can end war. It can, inside war, keep a little space where humans still want to keep rules and still admit the other exists.

That leads to the birth of the modern World Cup.

FIFA was founded in 1904, influence limited at first. The person who really pushed the World Cup up was Frenchman Jules Rimet. The name is a mouthful. We'll call him Old Lei.

Old Lei lived through World War I. After Europe was torn, he thought: does the human instinct to contest have to be released in war? Can you put that contest into a set of rules, let countries compete with matches, not guns?

That is the World Cup's first ideal. It did not exist at the start to sell broadcast rights, sponsors, tickets. The bottom idea: translate conflict into a match, compress violence into ninety minutes, put national feeling on a patch of grass.

In 1930, with Old Lei pushing, the first World Cup was in Uruguay. Conditions were poor. European teams complained of the distance, many would not take a ship to South America. Only 13 teams. Once the mechanism started, it was hard to stop.

Because the World Cup meets a very deep need: every country wants to be seen, every ordinary person wants, in a safe scene, to cheer for "us" once.

Then TV arrived, globalization, commercial sponsors. The World Cup went from an idealist international event to the world's largest attention machine.

The business model is simple.

The host spends. Stadiums, metro, airport, security, tourists, opinion pressure and operating risk. FIFA holds the core assets: the brand, broadcast, sponsorship, licensed goods, the rules and who may play.

So you see a strange thing: hosts often do not make money, even lose badly. Brazil spent a lot, some stadiums later barely used. Qatar spent astronomical sums, tickets and tourism cannot recoup it.

FIFA makes money. It does not bear the same cost for every metro, every stadium, every security detail, and it takes the bulk of global broadcast, ads, and sponsors.

Question: hosts know they may lose. Why scramble to host?

They are not buying football itself. They are buying national image.

Qatar hosted so the world would know it again: not only a rich energy mini-state, a Middle East diplomatic, commercial, cultural stage. Saudi wanting to host is similar logic: rebrand from traditional oil giant to modern tourism, entertainment, investment center.

So of course the World Cup has controversy. Corruption rumors, labor, human rights, huge waste, the power of a super-body like FIFA. At the same time it really is one of the few common events that can still hold a few billion people's attention at once.

That is the most complicated part.

One side very commercial, even very real. The other still keeps a shadow of idealism.

Like Iran standing on a U.S. pitch this time. You can say there is politics, calculation, FIFA interest, host pressure. You also cannot deny: when the whistle goes, the rules on the pitch briefly override the other rules. For those ninety minutes people watch not missiles, sanctions, and the table, but passes, shots, saves, and the score.

That may be why football exists to today.

Humans invented many things, and those things then shaped humans. We invented writing, writing gave us history. We invented money, money changed exchange and relations. We invented football, originally just to play, and it slowly became a ritual for all humanity.

A small ball, kicked around, looks light. It rolled over ancient grass, over World War I no-man's-land, over occupied Kiev, into today's World Cup pitch. It cannot make peace. It at least reminds us: as long as some people are willing to play under the same rules, humanity has not fully given up on each other.

So football is of course only football. And sometimes it is not only football.
