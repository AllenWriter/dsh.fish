Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. Ever travel and hit this: a lot of countries run 110V, so a three-pin thing like a hair dryer needs a converter, but a two-pin phone charger is fine. A modern day without electricity is basically unlivable.

This episode is electricity. Why civilian voltage split into 220V and 110V. There was a tech war in the middle. And those "ultra-high voltage" lines you hear in the news — what's that.

Before the story, a tiny recap. Voltage and current are a water-pipe system. Voltage is water pressure, current is flow speed, resistance is junk in the pipe. Pinch one end and you add pressure; the water runs faster. Higher voltage, faster current. Work around live power, wear insulating gloves. Those gloves have huge resistance. They block the current. A layer of protection.

Voltage's unit is the volt, V. Current is the ampere, A. Resistance is the ohm.

We used to laugh at Apple chargers being slow because they sat on 5V 1A for a long time. Same feeling at a charging pile: voltage too low, speed drops.

The world started on 110V.

In 1882 Edison builds the first DC grid in New York and lights 400 lamps at 110V. Why 110?

A few reasons. First, **materials**. Current travels on wire, so you need a lot of copper.

Copper was expensive. Voltage too low (50V) needed thicker copper. Voltage too high (200V) cooked the bulbs. 110V was the limit carbon-filament bulbs could take.

Like early phone chargers at 5V/1A — not because fast charge was impossible, because the battery and chip couldn't take it.

**Second, safety.** Early insulation was junk. 110V shock mortality about 3%; 200V jumped to 15%. Kids' toys use 3V batteries for the same reason: safety, not just tech.

**Third, distance.** DC can't be stepped up, and there were no transformers. Power left the plant at a relatively low voltage (110V) and went straight to the user. Distance from plant to user was capped, usually 1.6 km. The supply radius was small.

Like a spring on a hill. Only nearby villagers drink it. Farther away, the water doesn't get there; you walk over with a bucket. So a station couldn't sit more than 1.6 km from downtown. 110V was just enough for Manhattan's rich neighborhoods.

So DC 110V is not a great scheme. Too many limits. Another inventor, Nikola Tesla, steps up and in 1888 invents the AC system. Transformers easily lift AC above 10,000 volts for the trip, then drop it at the user. That's the system we still use.

Westinghouse takes the tech, lights 100,000 lamps at the Chicago World's Fair, 32 km of transmission. Insane for the time.

**Edison treats AC as the enemy.** A country's generation system can only keep one: DC or AC. Edison would rather spend every dollar he made in the DC era killing this new idea. To scare the public he electrocutes an elephant with AC, the "AC is dangerous" story; he even pushes New York to execute people with AC. That's where "electric chair" comes from.

Against a hard technical lead, the more efficient AC scheme still wins. Who wants to live next to the power plant?

There's a film about DC versus AC, The Current War. Recommend it.

Edison's 110V doesn't vanish. As a historical leftover it still runs in the U.S., Japan, and elsewhere. Some countries actually use 120V and still call it 110 out of habit.

As the tech matures, continental Europe mostly goes 220V, which heavily influences the Soviet Union. Some Republican-era concessions in China used 110V, but after 1949, facing 110 or 220, the engineers run the numbers:

- 110V from Shanghai to Beijing needs 87 substations;
- 220V cuts substations in half and copper use by 45%.

In 1956 China locks 220V as the standard. Grid build cost drops 30%, and it plants the seed for "west-to-east power."

The Soviets suggested 127V. Chinese engineers hold the line and pick 220V, which fits the land area. Today China has the world's longest ultra-high-voltage network, 48,000 km, moving as much electricity west to east each year as ten Three Gorges dams.

We built a pile of appliances, then electric cars, and demand keeps rising. So you keep lifting grid voltage. That's where UHV comes from: solar and wind in the western deserts become electricity for the east coast. Higher voltage, farther it can go.

Taking UHV seriously means China holds about 80% of global UHV patents, over 90% of equipment made at home. The obvious win: Chinese electricity is on the cheap end globally. OECD 35-country average residential rate is 1.35 yuan per kWh; China is 0.54. UK 2.67, Germany 1.96, U.S. 0.83.

Of course every country's power price wobbles.

By the way, remember Earth Hour, everyone switches lights off for an hour to waste less power? Later people stopped pushing it. Power plants don't actually shut down. The electricity they make mostly has to be used. If at peak hours the far end suddenly needs a lot less, the extra load can hurt the system. So those campaigns faded.
