Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. Last time we talked about why the world has 220V and 110V. This time we re-meet the inventor who keeps showing up in Chinese textbooks: the clever boy who used a mirror to help with his mother's surgery, the man who said genius is one percent inspiration and ninety-nine percent perspiration, the motivational idol with a thousand-plus patents. The story behind Edison.

That thousand-plus patents, domestic and foreign, is worth a chat by itself. A life is maybe thirty thousand days. To stack that many patents, Edison would have to invent one every thirty days. Is that a thing a human finishes?

The truth is simple. Edison had an invention company. Lots of labs, lots of researchers. Their day job was inventing new stuff and filing patents. Because Edison was the boss, a lot of collective projects landed under his name.

And those employees spent a lot of days watching what other inventors on earth were tinkering with. Peek east, peek west, whose progress looks promising. Edison Inc. had money and connections. A little extra effort, and it became an Edison project.

The most famous story is probably the light bulb.

The textbook says Edison ran thousands of experiments, all kinds of filament materials, even cat hair, comparing brightness, and finally invented the incandescent lamp.

In fact five or six people had a working bulb before him. The carbon filament that finally stuck was not even Edison's call. An Englishman named Swan decided that.

Edison probably felt a little guilty holding the patent, so he put up money and co-founded a company with Swan, to keep him quiet.

Edison was a businessman. He was close to the big commercial names. He went to the financier J.P. Morgan, talked him into investing, had the lab rush fifty thousand bulbs, and lit New York overnight.

That glittering night shocked the press and the public. Best ad of the age.

The real world is messy, and full of games. Dig into a simple light bulb, and the rules look a lot like how society still runs.

Besides inventing, Edison's business kept getting bigger. Once a person has power, we say they start to float.

After the bulb, the demand becomes power. On 4 September 1882, Thomas Edison starts the world's first commercial DC station on Pearl Street in New York. A 110V system, bronze pipes under the street, a 1.6-kilometer radius, five dollars a kilowatt-hour — about 136 dollars today. Voltage droops 8% per kilometer, but Edison General Electric still, with the full chain of bulb, generator, and distribution, owns 85% of the U.S. power market.

Edison hits a problem with a DC motor. His people cannot crack it. He says, whoever solves it gets fifty thousand dollars.

A young Serbian engineer named Nikola Tesla is technically hard enough. He grinds it out and solves it. He turns around to collect. Edison says you don't understand American humor. Tesla quits in a fury.

Tesla goes off and builds AC. Technically, AC is far ahead of DC. Tesla is good at the tech, and STEM guys have that death-grip on a problem. He does not have much money. He borrows if he has to. He also gives up patents — and thanks to that, we get cheap electricity.

DC's fatal weakness shows in 1884. Westinghouse gets Tesla's AC patents and builds the first practical AC grid in Pittsburgh. Transformers step up to 220V, 14,000-volt lines carry power across cities, unit cost drops to one-twentieth of Edison's. Even more shocking: at the 1893 Chicago World's Fair Tesla lights 250,000 AC lamps into an artificial day, a 34-kilometer radius.

STEM guys can do tech. Public opinion and the press are not Tesla's fight against Edison.

Facing a generation gap in the tech and a knot of interests, Edison launches the first systematic PR war in tech history. He funds a white paper of AC electrocutions — 728 cases, 87% of which were actually DC — and he steers the press to glue the electric chair to AC. At the first electrocution in Auburn Prison in 1890, Edison's lab even quietly supplies an improved AC generator.

His patent obsession reaches film. Early movies need a lot of big bulbs, and Edison already holds a pile of film patents. His company sits in New York, the East Coast. In the 1920s, if you want a cinema, or any business that touches film, you pay Edison's patent fees.

He works both sides of the street. An East Coast director who won't pay the steep fee does not just get sued. Local thugs come smash the place every few days.

So a lot of film companies run to the West Coast, California, and camp in a little town called Hollywood. Transport and telecom are weak. Edison's East Coast turf cannot reach that far. California judges know how touchy he is about patents; to protect local industry they tilt any case that touches his patents. American film blooms on the West Coast. A famous French director, Méliès, makes A Trip to the Moon, plans a U.S. release. Edison has an assistant secretly record it in France and show it in America. Méliès makes not a cent in the U.S. His company goes bankrupt. He sells candy in a train station.

Look closely and Edison is not only an inventor. He is a worldly commercial baron, and not a large-hearted one.

People are many-sided, and you have to see the era. Edison sits in America's Gilded Age, the roaring twenties, social Darwinism in the air. Winner takes all. Compete by any means. Winning is the victory.
