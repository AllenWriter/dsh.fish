## How Japan got rich

In 1980s Japan, someone would hold up a 50,000-yen note just to hail a cab. A five-minute ride cost 50,000 — ten thousand a minute.

That was crazy Japan in the eighties. Young people were bursting with energy. Compared with today's Heisei shut-ins, they might as well have been a different species.

Why? Back then, if you were a college grad, a job was waiting. You went to an interview, and before anyone even said hire or not, the company handed every candidate a souvenir. Companies had to keep up appearances, you know? Skip the gift and you looked rude. Word would get around the graduating class, and next year, who would even bother showing up?

So what if they don't show up? The company can still run, right? Not really. In those years, a perfectly ordinary Japanese firm could go under simply because it could not hire.

If you did get the offer, at least three months of training waited. You wanted to start contributing your genius right away? Sorry. I know you're in a hurry — sit tight. Soak in the company culture first. Learn how to use every allowance they give employees. At least two months of paid vacation a year. Fifteen months of pay, as a rule. Pension contributions: the highest tier, automatically.

Why treat people that well? Weren't they afraid you'd walk? As a noble kabushiki-gaisha employee, joining meant lifetime employment. Stack that against the decades until retirement, and this little upfront spend was nothing.

Benefits that good — was the work brutal? As one more screw in the machine, a lot of jobs just needed a body in the chair, even if you only signed papers or filled reports. Where did the confidence come from? In those years, who hadn't heard of Mitsubishi, Sony, Suzuki? Japanese cars, electronics, appliances sold worldwide without a fight. Make it, and someone would buy it. Japanese industrial goods poured into Europe and America like a tide. The printing press of money ran at whatever speed the factories could handle.

1980 to 1989 is still called the Showa boom. Japan's GDP grew about 8% a year, and listed companies, on average, were minting about ten million dollars of revenue a year. (That's listed-company data only.)

The stock market had its golden decade, about 10% a year. Market cap rose every year. Put in 10,000 dollars, take out a thousand.

And the yen kept climbing.

Japanese people traveled the world: March here, May there. Plenty of trips were company junkets. They had so much money they had basically invented every kind of allowance already.

Commute money and phone money were ordinary. Even wilder: there were allowances for, well, *xx*. The message was obvious. The company has too much cash. Please go find a way to spend it. Surplus sat on the books all year.

On TV, a reporter asked a boss how much a company should reasonably make in a year. He said, ten billion, sure? I can make a hundred million a minute.

That Japan had just ridden through the oil shock. The Western bosses were sick of high-fuel...

Company revenues went up every year.
