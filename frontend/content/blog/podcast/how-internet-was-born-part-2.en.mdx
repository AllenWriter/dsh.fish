Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. Last time we sketched how the internet was born — a military project, pushed by one engineer after another, until it was a tool anybody could touch. This episode: the internet and China, and the millennial bubble that popped.

Last time: in 1989 the Englishman Tim Berners-Lee invents the World Wide Web and the browser. How people get and share information flips. Ordinary people no longer need to learn heavy programming. A little browser holds more than a neighborhood library.

Once tech innovation starts, it grows its own will. The wind hits China fast, but plugging into the globe is not "just do it." Hardware first: root servers, undersea cables, and most of all, telecom people. Reform and opening is still early. The country needs training, and foreign experts.

The U.S. does not want to help at first. Germany sends a scientist named Werner Zorn to help China get on. In September 1987 he helps send the first email to the world: Across the Great Wall we can reach every corner in the world. In 1990 Professor Zorn helps register the CN top-level domain. China has its own internet name card.

That still is not full connection. In 1994 the U.S. National Science Foundation accepts China's request. A 64K international line opens. China is recognized as the 77th country with a full-function internet. 1994 is called China's internet year one.

Abroad, a PC is no longer astronomy. More American homes will buy a machine that can go online. From 1990 to 2000, PC ownership goes from 21.7% to 51%. That base makes internet companies possible.

A lot of those "products" are just a page. The door to the page is the browser. So a commercial war breaks out around browsers.

Remember Berners-Lee's browser? Crude. In 1993 Illinois students build Mosaic, the ancestor of the modern GUI browser. Core developer Marc Andreessen founds Netscape and ships Navigator, a hit.

That wakes Bill Gates. Microsoft builds Internet Explorer. From 1995, the famous browser war. Microsoft even bundles IE free with Windows. Netscape's share collapses.

How does it end? Netscape loses. The spirit doesn't. They open-source the browser. It becomes Firefox, which a lot of people still use.

Before the giants, the net is full of little sites.

Pages explode. People need a way to find things. In 1994 two Stanford grad students, Jerry Yang and David Filo, found Yahoo! At first it's a hand-edited directory, a giant yellow pages of the web.

Hand editing cannot keep up. In 1998 two more Stanford students, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, found Google. PageRank rewrites how search works: results that are actually relevant.

Imagine hunting information on the internet without search. Needle, ocean.

Besides browsing, can you do business? 1994, Jeff Bezos in a garage founds Amazon, first an online bookstore. 1995, Pierre Omidyar founds eBay, a place ordinary people can buy and sell.

That's e-commerce arriving. Suddenly you can buy the world without leaving home, even open a little shop online. Shopping changes.

Those new companies and products feel like today's AI wave, and Wall Street money notices. Yahoo, founded 1994, lists in two years and becomes the first internet company over a hundred billion dollars. Founders and managers are overnight nobles. Nobody wants to miss the next Yahoo.

From about '97 to 2000, any company with .com in the name gets bid to the sky. Even if the business doesn't make money, even if it barely touches the internet, cash still pours in. The bubbles get bigger.

1. **10 March 2000**: NASDAQ hits 5048.62. Widely called the peak of the internet bubble.

1. **March 2000–2001**: From March the market drops fast. The pop shows. Many internet companies without a sustainable model die or get bought.

1. **2001**: The bubble fully collapses. Nearly a thousand companies shut, more than 3,800 get merged. IT loses about five trillion dollars of market cap.

How bad? NASDAQ only stands back on that 5048 high in 2015. The bankrupt companies never wait that long.
