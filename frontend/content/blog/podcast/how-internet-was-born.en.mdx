Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. Going online is already an indispensable part of modern life. Assisted driving, this show, the programs on your phone and tablet — none of it without the internet. Today we ramble: how the internet was born, how a military project walked into millions of homes, and how it shapes modern society.

The story starts in World War II. After beating the Axis, the common enemy vanished, the Allies split. The U.S. and the Soviet Union, the two superpowers, became each other's number-one hypothetical enemy. Under the cloud of hostility, human creativity can fire. In the Cold War they competed hard in military and science. Dangerous, yes, and it also pushed big breakthroughs. The most striking: the birth of the internet.

When the Soviets launched the first satellite in 1957, America panicked. It meant the other side could already push force into space. A satellite is a lump of iron — what if they put a nuke on it? The U.S. was behind. That would not do.

So America moved fast. In 1958 it founded the Advanced Research Projects Agency, ARPA. One purpose: develop high military tech across the board, catch the Soviets.

America already had the world's first supercomputer. It quickly took ARPA's main compute. New machines arrived later, but each could only do its own job. Data between projects was isolated, no sharing. The military also wanted a distributed network: a way to keep data and communication alive through a nuclear strike. If one command center is hit, data is already backed up elsewhere, and the centers still talk. Like a spiderweb: every key node 100% online.

In 1962, communications expert J. C. R. Licklider was invited into ARPA as the first director of the information-technology office. His job: find a way to connect the computers, and build a talent team for that distributed-network need. It was not easy.

Not until 1969 — later than the moon landing — did university experts find a reliable computer-to-computer method. Computers cannot just pick up a phone. There was no unified OS. People who knew programming languages were rare, mostly military and research. And to make them talk, the wiring itself was a problem.

October 29, 1969. An exciting day in internet history. 10:30 p.m., the first ARPANET connection test. One end UCLA, the other Stanford Research Institute, 500 kilometers away. The task: send one word from Los Angeles to Stanford: login.

We typed L. Did you get it? Got it. Stanford staff could not hold the excitement.

Typed O. Did you get it? It came through. Hands shaking.

Typed G. The system crashed. Transmission cut.

Humanity's first internet connection sent only two letters, L and O. The door to a new era was already open.

In 1969 ARPANET had four nodes, four universities: UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara, Utah. Four campus computers talking. Nodes grew fast: 15 in 1971, 213 in 1981.

A new problem: every university had a computer, but not the same model. There was no idea of a general-purpose machine. A computer was usually built for one job — storage, ballistics. Imagine each of today's apps as its own computer, only that task. Talking across kinds became a huge problem. If computers are buildings in a virtual world, the roads between them were dirt tracks, no navigation. You walk until you stop, you don't know if the road even goes through. Throughput was tiny.

As more machines joined, the primitive NCP protocol really could not hold. Like a village where everyone knows everyone and talks fine, then a crowd of newcomers arrives with different languages and cultures. Communication breaks. To talk together you invent a language — a protocol — and standardize.

In 1974 Robert Kahn and Vint Cerf proposed TCP/IP, the way we go online today, the start of the internet's grand unification. When homes first got net, people said "dial-up." That process is asking the carrier for an IP, plugging in a modem, and the house is on.

An IP is a unique street number for every computer that wants to join. Usually four dots, like 1.1.1.1. TCP handles the data: if something breaks, signal, retransmit, until it is all there.

After nearly a decade, the idea went live across ARPANET in 1983. Note: it is still ARPANET, not "the internet," because it was not fully open to ordinary people. Early ARPANET was closed, mostly military and research. Purpose was strong: military or academic. A small-circle product. The military had not left. Commercial computers were banned. An invention this good, more people learn about it, commercial internet demand gets urgent. ISPs are born: Internet Service Providers, access. China Mobile, Unicom, Telecom — those operators are ISPs too.

Every phone, every computer that wants to go online applies to a carrier for an IP, then goes out through base stations or switches.

So the early net stayed in a few hands. Not until 1989 did the British Tim Berners-Lee, wanting ordinary people to feel the charm, experiment after experiment, code after code, invent the World Wide Web — www — and the browser. A global information system. Any ordinary person online can find shared information through a browser. Human efficiency jumped. Information speed entered the "speed of light" era.

The internet changed not only how information moves, but the global economy. It gave new industries soil, birthed giants: Google, Amazon, Alibaba. They created huge economic value and keep pushing the tech.
