Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. When did you last eat Japanese food? I got a craving over the weekend, treated myself to sushi, and ordered a plate of salmon. The second it landed I couldn't help thinking: how did this fish get so expensive? I looked it up, and even the Chinese name "sanwenyu" has a Cantonese story.

This episode is the salmon story — how it became a cultural symbol of a high-end life.

What do you see when someone says salmon? A warm little izakaya? A precise sushi platter in a fancy room? That oily mouthfeel, or that loud orange-red?

Salmon, also called 鲑鱼, has a habit: a fierce loyalty to the place it was born. The eggs hatch in a clear freshwater river. After a while the young fish swim to the sea. Every summer they have to dodge the big hunters out there — whales, for one. A pod of 300 orcas can take a thousand tons of salmon in a day.

After escaping those mouths they finally hit the rivers again, back in fresh water. The body goes through a pile of physiological changes to switch from salt to fresh. Gills and kidneys rebalance salt. Color changes. That's osmoregulation.

The point is to get back to the exact creek of their birth and spawn. The kids do the same loop: ocean, then home. They've lived like this for fifty million years. Their reproductive strategy is the most tragic kind of evolutionary math — a sea of fish. Along the way they feed the rest of nature, including us.

The Jurchen people in the northeast forests learned early how to catch this fish in the rivers. Every summer, when the salmon came home to spawn, they held a sacred rite and shouted damaha. They believed the fish would return on time in autumn — heaven's winter food.

Even in Cixi's time, the last dish of the New Year's Eve meal was a plate of dumplings. That was the ancestors' tradition, and the filling had to be damaha — today's chum salmon. Yes, salmon and damaha are both salmonids, just different oceans: Pacific salmon versus Atlantic salmon.

What we usually mean by "sanwenyu" today is Atlantic salmon from Norway. How did that food get this status?

Go back to 1885. A cabinet of fresh Norwegian salmon arrives in Guangzhou. Cantonese people ask what fish this is. The Norwegians quote the European Latin and say Salmon — a fish that leaps. Guangzhou hears the English, and in Cantonese introduces it to the rest of the country as 三文鱼. Very fancy. You can eat it raw.

The same Norwegians keep sailing east, hit Japan, and find the Japanese much harder to hustle. On board is a fisheries official named Bjørn Eirik Olsen — wait, the script calls him Listo — who'd come a decade earlier trying to sell Norwegian seafood to Japan and been shown the door. Japan was a fishing power. It didn't need imports.

By 1985 Japan is the world's second economy, and domestic seafood only covers about 50% of demand.

The Norwegians smell it. They whisper: we brought a boat of high-end sashimi fish, perfect for the mouthfeel you like. The Japanese are interested. Layer after layer of packaging comes off. The Japanese look: this is just salmon. Aristocrats here have not eaten raw salmon for thousands of years. Parasites, don't you know? And it's not even fancy. We have it in our rivers. Don't try to make us import it.

Listo goes home with a bloody nose and tries another door. He hires a Norwegian ad firm, hoping marketing can blast the Japanese market open. The brand line: Norwegian seawater is clean, all natural. Salmon grown in icy water, no parasites.

Even so, Japanese salmon imports don't explode until 1995. That year Japan's economy starts sliding, and Norway is sitting on a salmon glut, desperate to dump at floor prices. The Norwegians find a Japanese company that sells food for ordinary people — Nishidai, more or less, moving frozen dumplings, chicken nuggets, cheap squid.

That's when salmon gets turned into sushi and put on a shelf. Nobody expected that orange-red to pull Japanese shoppers so hard, or that oily sashimi mouthfeel to be this popular. In a slump, who says no to a cheap, nutritious box? Wait — weren't Japanese people supposed to hate raw salmon?

They probably pitched the wrong room. Japanese aristocrats and Japanese commoners are not the same crowd.

Drunk on Japanese yen, the Norwegians immediately eye China. This time they're even more confident, even more superior: look, eating salmon raw is a tradition in Japan and Norway. Very high-end. Don't you dare order too much.

And so salmon starts sprinting in China, into the unshakable status it has now.

Can you actually eat salmon raw? That "tradition" was mostly a Norwegian export. Anywhere in the world that has salmon, except Norway, people know it can carry parasites and you don't eat it raw.

Any animal with a freshwater chapter in its life has a shot at parasites — crayfish, crabs, you don't eat those raw either. Saltwater fish can be eaten raw because the osmotic pressure is high and parasites are fewer. Every salmon starts in fresh water and only later goes to sea.

So is all raw salmon off-limits? Not quite. Food-safety advice in a lot of countries is: freeze at minus twenty for at least 24 hours. Or just eat the roe — the odds of parasites in the eggs are lower.
