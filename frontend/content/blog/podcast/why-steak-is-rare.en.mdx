Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. This episode: steak.

How would you like that cooked? Probably the most confusing question a Chinese diner gets in a Western restaurant. It's beef, right? Shouldn't they just cook it through and bring it out? Why am I being asked how done?

In that world, doneness is five steps by center temperature: rare, medium-rare, medium, medium-well, well. Europeans and Americans are almost fanatical about the cut that is seared outside, pink inside, tender, still leaking red juice — medium-rare to medium-well. When steak hits a Chinese table, medium-well with no "blood" is most people's floor.

So why skip well-done for "half-raw"? And how is steak different from ordinary beef?

Picture the eating: a pan-fried steak, knife and fork on each side. Fork pins it, serrated knife cuts small pieces, red juice runs, you chew slowly. The whole motion is smooth. Elegant, yes.

That "stereotype of civilized steak" is actually young. It only spreads in the early 20th century. Steak starts as Scandinavian steak, a thick slice on a grill. In the Middle Ages nobody fusses about cut or manners: meat on open fire, ordinary people eat with their hands — yes, steak begins as hand food. The table fork comes much later in Europe.

Today "steak" means higher-grade beef from loin and rump, cut thick, grilled or pan-fried. Even the idea that steak is better than "just beef" has been quietly planted. That "steak is fancier" impression is commerce plus table manners.

Late 19th century, transcontinental railroads stitch Atlantic to Pacific. Chicago slaughterhouses' marbled beef takes over America. Beef is slowly sculpted into a food of strength, masculinity, race (in that social context), and economic success. By flavor, tenderness, juiciness, maturity, and marbling, from canned scraps to snowflake steaks, the U.S. builds a grading system. Only the top three — Prime, Choice, Select — fat roughly 3–11%, fine and tender, are steak-worthy. On a cow, those cuts sit mainly above the spine, both sides.

Western livestock is not for plowing. It's milk and meat. Muscle along the spine mostly controls bending, not hard labor, so fibers are finer. Breeding meat cattle also lifts fat in the back. Faced with marbled, tender steak, clever butchers drop the old "blast it well-done" method and pick pan-fry/grill from fry, roast, stew, boil — the ways that wake fat aroma and keep mouthfeel. They carve the most tender tenderloin and the sirloin, give them pretty names, pair them with French dining ritual, and bind steak to class: a stiff napkin, engraved silver, a tannic old Barolo. A rough roast completes its class jump.

Why do Westerners like it half-raw? Meet **myofibrillar protein**. It runs contraction, water-holding, hardness, tenderness. Studies on cooking doneness say: as center temperature rises, the protein surface gets more hydrophobic. Plainly: better at locking juice in. Myoglobin and pigments start to denature. A metal pan as heat, high temperature runs Maillard on the surface, the inside stays pink, contrast of texture and flavor. That window is roughly medium-rare to medium-well, juicy and tender. Past about 65°C (past medium-well), those proteins seize, the meat wrings like a sponge, juice gone, chewy and dry. No wonder well-done feels like work.

Funny: the medium-well Chinese diners prefer sits right on the denaturation edge. Avoid the red juice that makes people uneasy, keep a lot of the tenderness.

For many older Chinese people steak is unfamiliar; in Chinese cooking, steak with the vest off is **tenderloin** or **fillet**. The usual idea: slice, strip, mince, then blast in oil or dunk in boiling water, cooked more through.

So if medium and medium-well taste best, why no traditional "thick, half-raw" pan steak in Chinese food? Maybe shear force in Chinese yellow cattle. Tenderness in the mouth is "easy to bite, little leftover." In the lab it's **shear force**: higher, less tender. Studies put the most common Chinese yellow cattle around **8 kg**, Angus around **4 kg**. Frankly, yellow cattle are less tender than typical meat breeds.

That's also civilization's resource mix. China farmed intensively. Cattle were first an efficient plow — almost a high-load tractor. "Cattle are the root of farming." You raise them to work, not to eat. For a long time privately slaughtering a plow ox was banned. Ordinary people rarely ate beef; if they did, it was often an old, worked-to-death ox. Heavy plowing needs strong yellow cattle. Day after day, muscle grows, fibers get thick and long — good for work, rough to eat. Even the tenderest loin is no exception. Look at old "ox plowing" pictures: the neck and back do the work, so the longissimus — including the loin area — is a main load zone.

Meat breeds like Angus and wagyu, before slaughter, either graze and fatten or eat feed in a stall. They barely work, to keep fibers fine. Breeding aims differ: China selected strong, hardworking yellow cattle; the West selected tender meat; plus a life of farm work that further weakens the eating quality.

Still, as the country that "knows how to eat," even with a weaker hand, Chinese cooking can make it good. The idea above: **slice the loin thin**, then **hot-oil stir-fry** or **hot-water dunk**, shorter heat, less protein squeeze, less juice loss. Signature dishes: stir-fried yellow-cattle beef, Chaoshan beef hotpot. Same exam — how to make beef tender — East and West turn in two delicious papers: the West in "thick cut plus precise heat," the East in "knife work plus fast cook."

Now, as American steak culture spreads, thick-cut steak is common in China too. When in Rome. People who can't do classic "half-raw" invent "new Chinese steak": marinate before the pan, a thin starch coat to lock water, or just drop it in hotpot with a dipping sauce. Whatever the method, whatever the cut, when we lift a medium-well compromise or try a medium-rare dare, in the clink of knife, fork, and chopsticks, maybe that's the nicest resonance: the hunt for extreme flavor always outruns culture and time, and maps onto wanting a good life.
