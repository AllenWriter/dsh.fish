Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. I'm your host, Qiao Yulan. New Year is almost here — an early greeting to all the grown-ups and kids. If you ask what people look forward to most besides red envelopes, it has to be fireworks. Yes: today's box is the star that lights the night sky, the story behind fireworks and gunpowder.

Since February I've been in Spring Festival mode. Symptom: no heart for work, only for holiday, then firecrackers, heh. Why "then"? Have you been sneaking fireworks in Shenzhen, where they're banned?

Of course not. This New Year's Eve I drove the ET5 with friends to a villa by the sea in Huizhou, and we let fireworks rip on the beach. The scene was gorgeous. The whole sand, everyone's fireworks competing, one act after another, the beach no longer black but lit by round after round of bloom. At midnight, every color lit every face. We sat in the new year together. That feeling is too good.

Year-flavor is not only the smell of reunion dinner. It is the gunpowder in the air after you light fireworks, lion dance in the ears, crackers. The old line: in the sound of crackers a year is sent off, spring wind warms the Tusu wine.

I had a kid's question: why are crackers also called 爆竹, exploding bamboo? Gunpowder and bamboo shouldn't be related. Right: bamboo is neither tool nor ingredient. The only link is the sound — both go pop-pop-pop, viciously loud. So does New Year need noise? Yes. The point of 过年 is to drive off "Nian," the beast, the evil thing. Make noise, scare it away. Ancients invented every kind of racket to chase spirits and bad luck. Today: the story behind crackers and gunpowder.

Before gunpowder, people found by accident that heating bamboo is extremely loud. No need to bang gongs, and you get a fire for warmth. The method spread. To this day we still call crackers 爆竹.

In the seventh century, bamboo's fate was about to be replaced by real crackers. Early Tang, thanks to Taizong Li Shimin's reforms, the Zhenguan era. The country was rich, and it encouraged many cultures. Daoism, Buddhism, Confucianism ran together, a bit of a hundred schools.

Tang was the Li family's dynasty. Laozi of Daoism was also a Li. Naturally the Lis promoted Daoism. Peace, grain, people started wanting the spiritual. The Daoist crowd grew. They sat and talked, held debate contests. More important: alchemy.

Yes. Tang had too many rich people, court to street, everyone coveted immortality. Now you know why the Tang monk in Journey to the West was such a prize. There was a real prototype. To cultivate, you refine pills. Demand for immortality made a lot of Daoists obsessed with the furnace.

Sometime a recipe spread in that circle: mix niter, sulfur, and carbonized saponin seeds, light it, it burns violently. Nobody knows who found it first. Some say it came down from the Five Dynasties. If you want history to remember you, write it down. The Tang alchemist Qingxu first recorded a primitive gunpowder method in a secret elixir text, so later people could follow the recipe. Gunpowder left the small Daoist circle, craftsmen took it, made crackers for Tang's prosperous festivals, extra luck.

Someone will say: typical peace-loving Chinese civilization, such a powerful thing, not for war, for fireworks. Lu Xun had a similar sigh: foreigners make bullets with gunpowder; China makes crackers to honor the gods.

Lu Xun's point was to spur the country. On this sentence he was a bit one-sided. Chinese people put gunpowder into weapons early. In the 1161 Song–Jin battle at Caishi, the first explosive gunpowder, the "thunderbolt bomb," appeared. After it blew it dumped smoke, hid troop movement. In 1259 at Diaoyu Fortress, Southern Song cannon even killed Möngke, slowing the Mongol plan to swallow Eurasia.

The real East–West arms gap may sit on two things: walls and armor. The East did not have very hard man-made fortresses or sky-high stone walls. Often a ladder or a siege was enough. If you needed engines, a trebuchet could already tear a small mouth in the wall. No need to invent cannon to replace it.

Eastern war used huge numbers of people. Many soldiers were farmers who could not buy expensive armor, so the bow was enough. Before the bullet, early muskets loaded and fired like snails. A decent archer could send twenty arrows a minute. A musketeer still had to pour powder, seat the ball, raise, aim. Three shots was already good.

The West sprinted up the heavy-cannon skill tree. Europe had too many hard forts. Constantinople's wall was 12 meters — four stories — and 5 meters thick, two stories of thickness. To break that with human trebuchets, just moving the stones would eat craftsmen. Toss a stone in the air and you still cannot guarantee it hits the weak point.

That problem birthed the demand for serious cannon. Cannon then was: make the stone faster, and make it accurate. Ten stones on one weak point, someday it breaks.

To fire a stone with enough force you need iron that can take the chamber pressure. To hit, you grind math and ballistics. Cast iron got better. By the 16th century they could cast a one-piece barrel. Firing got safer, the punch bigger. Infantry armor and shields hardened with the iron, and the bow started to fall short.

Modern ballistics took shape. In 1537 Tartaglia published the first shooting-theory book, describing fire with math.

While Western military work was arguing with scientific thinking, the East still explained arms with metaphysics. After seeing Western firearms in the age of sail, Xu Guangqi and others translated Western books in bulk. Sun Yuanhua brought in Western military tech in Xifa Shenji. Sadly China then had no basic-science frame, so they had to explain with yin-yang five-phases.

Europe itself had no unified units. After they arrived in the East, conversion error made the numbers unusable. Build a gun that way and it bursts on the field. Soldiers drop an experiment that wild. Nobody jokes with their own life.

Without empirical science, ordinary literati could not describe arms accurately. The prose went as exaggerated as it could.

Jinshi on the early bomb "heaven-shaking thunder": the blast "heard a hundred li." Wubeizhi on a hand gun: three qian of powder wounds the enemy at three hundred paces, men and horses shot clean through, several at once.

The "mouth-cannon" of the literati showed its holes the moment frontline generals used it. Qiu Jun said a "divine spear" fired a hundred paces, the enemy was hit as they heard the shot. In Qi Jiguang's hands, in real combat, the arrows flew crooked, sometimes butt-first. Nearly scrap.

The gap grew. In modern times East and West met on the field with their own arms. The brutal score is why Lu Xun said that hate-the-iron-for-not-becoming-steel line. If you do not see the bottom principle, a moment of muddle and cleverness can buy short gain. Sooner or later the shape shows.
