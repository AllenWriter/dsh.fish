# Diamonds are forever. De Beers is not necessarily

Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box.

Today a topic that fits wedding season: diamonds.

If you roll time back five years, a couple getting married, buying rings, would probably hit a soul question.

Should we grit our teeth and go for a diamond ring?

If you don't buy, ceremony feels missing.

If you buy, the wallet quietly alarms beside you.

Today the question is suddenly less tangled. A lot of people think: a gold ring is fine too. Holds value, you can see it, touch it, and you don't start suspecting you got marketing-handled the moment you take it home.

Funner: while gold prices keep walking up, and people start believing gold again, diamond prices on the other side keep walking down.

Who is most embarrassed?

Not the jewelry store.

Not the wedding company.

The super-giant that once almost defined the modern diamond industry: De Beers.

The name you may not know. The sentence you definitely know.

A diamond is forever.

Friends, that is not an ordinary slogan.

It is more like a spell.

It turned a transparent stone into proof of love; a mineral into a ticket to marriage; a tradable good into an emotional souvenir you had better not resell second-hand.

This magic, De Beers used for almost a century.

Now the magic is failing.

In recent years De Beers revenue and profit have both slid, inventory of rough over two billion dollars. At some 2025 auctions, sightholders even refused to take the goods.

What does that mean?

Before, people queued to grab. Now you put the goods out, they look, and say: sorry, I don't want them.

For a company that eats by controlling supply, this is not ordinary cooling.

This is faith loosening.

Why did De Beers get here?

The story starts in South Africa more than a hundred years ago.

Late nineteenth century, diamond mines found in Kimberley.

At first, of course, excitement.

Diamonds: wealth, scarcity, a fate lottery bursting from underground.

The problem came fast.

Too many mines.

Capital rushed in, mine owners rushed in, machines rushed in. Digging went from small-scale to industrial. Output exploded in a short window, prices smashed down.

Note, here is a counter-intuition.

The real enemy of diamonds was never poverty.

The real enemy is too many.

Once people find the thing is not as rare as they imagined, the sacred skin peels at once.

Then a British merchant appeared.

Cecil Rhodes.

Long name. We'll call him Old Luo.

Old Luo saw one thing: if you want to make big money on diamonds, you cannot dig as hard as you can.

Everyone digs, price collapses.

The real way to earn is to grab every tap in your own hand.

So when other small owners were beaten near bankrupt by low prices, Old Luo did not panic with the market. He started buying mines cheap.

One today.

Merge one tomorrow.

By 1888 he folded what he had into De Beers Consolidated Mines.

From day one De Beers was not an ordinary mining company.

It was more like the central bank of the diamond world.

An ordinary company thinks: how do I sell the product?

De Beers thinks: how do I decide how many diamonds the world should see?

To do that it built a very overbearing central selling system.

Simply: rough from a huge share of world mines cannot be sold to whoever, at whatever price. It goes into De Beers, graded, priced, allocated as one.

The core logic is simple.

Diamond price cannot be left to a free market.

A free market is too honest.

Supply up, it cuts price.

Consumers don't buy, it cuts price.

De Beers wanted to cut diamonds off from that market.

Like a dam between mine and consumer.

How much water upstream, it knows.

How much to release downstream, it decides.

If a mine owner disobeys, wants to sell cheap around the system, De Beers can use its own stock, start a price war, beat you until you submit.

Not enough.

De Beers also invented the sightholder system.

What is a sightholder?

Think of them as wholesale clients De Beers has certified.

The certification is not to make you God.

The opposite: to make you obey.

At a fixed time, fixed place, De Beers puts a packet of diamonds in front of you: this goods, this price, this quantity.

You can look.

Basically you cannot bargain.

You cannot say I only want the good ones, not the bad.

Still less can you refuse when the market is bad.

If you don't cooperate, next time you may not be on the list. High-quality supply gets hard.

Friends, this is not ordinary business.

It is more like a club.

De Beers is the person at the door with the list.

Want in, keep the rules.

Don't keep them, don't play.

On this system De Beers once controlled over 80% of world diamond distribution.

What does that mean?

It was not simply selling diamonds.

It was defining diamonds.

What kind is worth money.

When to release goods.

Who is qualified to buy.

Even how consumers should understand diamonds.

Supply control alone is not enough.

Because diamonds have a natural problem.

They are not grain. Skip a day and you starve.

Not a house. People have to live.

Not gold, which everyone has taken as a wealth sign for thousands of years.

To sell diamonds high you must answer: why must I buy.

De Beers's answer: because of love.

In 1947 that famous line appeared.

A diamond is forever.
