Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. I'm your host, Qiao Yulan. We continue: how the atomic bomb was made. Back to 1939, nerves taut. German scientists had found fission. The German army had orders to scrape materials for a bomb.

President Roosevelt, after Einstein's warning letter, unhurriedly set up an advisory committee and called U.S. nuclear physicists. The meeting's verdict: fission can be weaponized. And then? Then nothing.

Einstein, seeing that, was furious. He felt ignored, and never joined the committee. He also disliked bomb research, thinking it would destroy humanity.

You cannot entirely blame Roosevelt. America was not in the war. To start a bomb you need money. Money means Congress. Once you open that exploration, you burn at least billions, and it might fail. In peacetime even an extra carrier is hard. A project that looks like a bottomless pit is harder.

Szilard, who wrote the letter with Einstein — the Hungarian from last episode who talked Einstein into America — did not lie down. He found Fermi, the great physicist Italy's fascists had also scared off. Szilard ran around raising money and buying materials. Fermi buried himself in the lab. In the committee's name they started a reactor.

If the bomb is a supercar with no drawings and an unknown route, the first job is to study a two-wheel bicycle, add functions, optimize in practice. Vehicle motion is the same idea; only speed and power differ.

If the bomb is a huge explosive, this little reactor is a small firecracker. Same principle. Go from 0 to 1, then calculate what material, what mass, to set it off.

Power shows in control. You need it quiet in transit, then bursting at the right time and place, to scare the opponent as hard as possible.

Scientists and civilians alone were still small-time, hard to make a big result. After Pearl Harbor, America entered the war, war potential fully mobilized. Weapon research got huge budgets. The reactor was listed a national key project and moved into a basement at the University of Chicago. Near-unlimited resources lifted the team's morale. In December 1942 they lit it, controlled fission. That day humanity entered the atomic age.

Minimum viable verified. Make the bomb. Roosevelt put the U.S. Army in full charge. The Army said we can fight; engineering belongs to the engineers. Engineer HQ was in Manhattan, so call it the Manhattan Project.

They found General Leslie Groves, then building the Pentagon. Old Luo, you built the Pentagon so pretty, a bomb will be no problem. What, a house-builder making a bomb?

Besides nuclear physics, the plan included secretly manufacturing weapons at scale. Those three conditions mean several large bases, military management. Efficiency and secrecy. The boss must be an officer who understands engineering.

Engineering lead in place, still need an R&D lead. Groves looked at nuclear physicists in America. Many had fled Europe. Szilard had done a lot, but he was not American. National security: pick an American, preferably stars-and-stripes born. Who brought quantum mechanics back? Oppenheimer.

Oppenheimer was born in a wealthy Jewish family in New York. Wide interests from childhood, taught himself French and chemistry. Fame among peers, a prodigy. Growing in flowers and applause, he became proud, strict with himself, disgusted by what he could not do well.

After Harvard undergrad in 1925 he flew to Cambridge. The world's academic center was still Europe. Harvard knowledge was not enough. He had to cram theory and math. Cambridge physics loved experiment, not his strength. He learned fast, hands were weaker. Every lab session, a few smashed tubes.

Study abroad plus repeated failure gave him serious mental illness. In 1926 he met Bohr, a master of quantum mechanics, visiting Cambridge. Bohr offered a branch: you don't like the lab, come to Germany and do theory with me. Little Mo wrote in his diary, very happy: I am very glad to get out of laboratory work. I never do well there; others are unsatisfied, I am uninterested; I feel I am only forced to do it.

After a German doctorate he taught at Berkeley. As a teacher he was excellent. He taught theory, then branched into many fields. A merchant-family son, he dressed well, took students to high-end restaurants. He soon had student fans who copied his speech and manners.

Groves looked at the resume and already had an answer. One small problem: the kid was red. Brother, sister-in-law, lover, all Communist Party. He had not joined, but often helped organize and donate. The Soviets were Allies, but this was state secret.

Emergency. Groves trusted his eye and backed Oppenheimer as R&D lead.

He explained Manhattan. Oppenheimer accepted fast and advised: concentrate force, don't let scientists fight separately like Germany. How? Build a city in the New Mexico desert. Scientists bring families, live there. Secrecy solved as a bonus.

Groves agreed, led 130,000 people, and in months built a western town. Today it is Los Alamos National Laboratory, still making cutting U.S. weapons.

Oppenheimer recruited the country's top scientists. Not easy. Anyone who has managed a big company knows: the bigger the case, the bigger the talent, and big talent has big temper.

During the bomb, every top U.S. scientist was drafted. Nobel winners everywhere. Even if you put Einstein in charge, they might not submit.

Oppenheimer was not yet 40, no Nobel. He spoke eight languages, a wide knowledge, so he had no barrier with scientists who had fled Europe, and he understood their specialties. Rarer: expert-level chemistry, metallurgy, weapons production, engineering, systems management. He also showed up in the lab and answered needs at once.

As logistics boss, Groves did his best to feed Oppenheimer's crazy ideas. To build a large cyclotron they needed a huge magnetic coil, about 80 meters across, a lot of copper.

America had already poured almost all copper into war production. No pure copper left. Even the landlord has no spare grain.

What then? Oppenheimer said, no copper, silver works. Physically silver conducts better than copper.

This is silver. Groves did not hesitate. He sent an assistant to Treasury to borrow it.

The assistant told Under Secretary Bell the request, refused to say the use, only that it was for the war.

Treasury asked how much. Assistant Nichols: 6,000 tons. 6,000 tons? Not cement, precious metal. Treasury tried to stall, said you may not grasp weight. Groves insisted. In the end Treasury put out 14,700 tons of silver. Almost 100% later returned to the vault. Groves's salary was 600 dollars a month. Every day he decided millions of dollars of flow.

Thanks to Groves and Oppenheimer's efficient systems, and many scientists, three years later the first bomb, Fat Man, bloomed in the desert. Side note: that blast changed wine on Earth forever.

Grapes absorb carbon dioxide as they grow, and carbon isotopes. After 1945 many countries tested bombs. Those explosions put carbon-14 in the air for the first time. Grapes absorbed it; it ended in the wine.

Harmless to people, easy to detect. Any wine made after 1945 will contain that isotope.
