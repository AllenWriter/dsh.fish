Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. Last episode we talked about the Trump family rise, briefly the grandfather, and how the father entered real estate and set Donald's world.

This episode we keep digging the growth story: how a free-range rich second generation became the family-business boss.

How the Trumps went from a local clan to one of the top families in America.

Donald was born in 1946, the second son. Father Fred Trump, with fat connections, had already built a lot of apartments and houses in New York's so-called "poor districts," Queens and Brooklyn.

In today's words, a proper landlord. That gave Donald a carefree childhood. Dad put the inheritance hope on the eldest, Fred Jr. Donald had almost no requirements, so from small he had a superiority and confidence beyond other people, very wild.

At 13 dad sent him to New York Military Academy. First time he learned how to meet rules, and how to obey. Most important, it trained him to meet challenge. After the academy he went to the famous Wharton, then back to dad's company.

Young, handsome vice president of the Trump Organization — already the male lead in a short drama about a tyrant CEO.

Still not dad's first-choice heir. He badly needed a campaign to prove himself. The 1970s: the group hit trouble. A batch of public apartments dad had built, Trump Village, allegedly treated applicants differently by skin and race, marking a C for color. Someone reported it. The government sued. If they paid a huge settlement to make it go away, the group would wear a racism hat forever, later business would be dead, maybe bankrupt overnight.

Donald panicked, hunted for help. He went to New York's famous 21 Club, the playground every respectable person wanted into. With a million dad had funded, he finally knocked the door. Worth it: the Kennedy political clan, the richest Vanderbilts, publishing tycoon Newhouse.

Still a long way from those stars. A nobody, kids' table. For a 25-year-old, he still loved the air. He told his date he would be a billionaire. The gene and the conditions, he had them.

Bored, he felt a strange stare from a far booth, wolf, hawk, beast, devil. A man named Russell invited him over. Instinct said no. Hearing old member, hunger for the powerful, he went.

Sat down and saw: America's most evil lawyer, Roy Cohn. Born 1927. Bright from small. Prosecutor at 23. In a famous 1950 spy case, with the only evidence, he convicted a couple and sent them to the chair.

In 1953, this 26-year-old legal prodigy became Senator Joseph McCarthy's chief counsel, de facto chief designer of McCarthyism, a red scare against progressives. Many innocents ruined, some suicide. He never showed a shred of regret.

Now Roy was New York's famous white glove, cleaning trouble for the powerful. After small talk he found Donald was Fred's son, knew the group's hole. His advice: sue the government, never admit the charges. Donald saw hope, asked if Roy would take the case. Roy laughed it off as a joke.

Life went on. Sued by the government, Trump Village became a soft persimmon everyone could squeeze. Tenants found excuses to skip rent. Donald went door to door. Hard. Everyone thought the family was finished. Nobody took the second son seriously.

At family dinner he proposed asking dad to hire Roy. Dad thought the man a con lawyer, sued by the feds three times. The eldest, a pilot, tried to help: don't be so biased. That made dad explode: my son is just a bus driver with wings, then insult after insult.

Dad had always pinned succession on the eldest. The brother was warm, generous, kind, everyone liked him — fatal in business. He ended as an airline pilot. Mother Mary rushed to break it up: home is not for business. The furious brother dragged Donald to a bar, had to get drunk. That drinking habit killed him at 42.

Donald was strict with himself, never indulged, clear goals, no tobacco, alcohol, or forbidden stuff.

Besides the lawsuit he had a dream: buy the famous Commodore Hotel, next to Grand Central where tens of thousands commute, Manhattan's busiest stretch. Not enough money. He found dad's old friend to raise funds. Flat no. What Fred would not agree, a friend would not. Besides, New York crime was high; no guests would come to such a mess.

After repeated begging the friend showed the bottom card. Unless the group settled the lawsuit, nobody would partner.

Fine. Two things became one.

The person Donald could think of was that one-meeting lawyer Roy. After a long wait the man came. Donald formally asked Roy to take the case. Roy had lost interest, walked into the bathroom. Never refused before, Donald felt cornered for the first time, put down the proud head. Please, Mr. Roy, only you can fight these bullies.

The word bully moved Roy a little. They sat. To Roy, the government was short of money, squeezing capitalists. Who bothers with so-called civil-rights campaigns on a normal day? Still the 70s. America had only just ended 60s segregation.

So the case could be big or small. Not what law the group broke. Who the judge was. After they opened up, Donald told Roy the dream: buy the dying Commodore, make it a New York landmark again. Roy got interested. Suddenly the kid in front of him felt like the same species.

Next day Donald went to Roy's apartment and, without asking, gave Roy the million dad had given him as startup money. He saw photos of Roy with big names, asked how he knew so many. Roy: the feds sued me three times, I walked each time. I am a winner. People only fawn on winners.

Roy also told his winning rules. First: attack, attack, keep attacking.

He called the civil-rights lawyer the government had hired, a mouth of filth, demanded they drop the case or he would countersue for a hundred million. Justice Department called Roy at once, said they would deal with him someday. Roy denied he had ever made the call.

Second: admit nothing, deny everything. On first instance it showed full. Roy grabbed the lack of hard evidence, tangled, never admitted. He told Donald: the key is not the court, it is the person in the courtroom, Justice's Walter. Fix him, they drop it.

In Roy's view, the letter-marking was already un-flippable. Donald panicked. Roy: remember the third rule. Whatever they say, declare your victory, never admit defeat, never surrender.

Roy privately found Walter, used evidence of an affair to force a withdrawal. Donald won, not very glorious. Roy said, earnest: you play baseball? On the field the ball matters, right? In real society, it is always the person. Even in a country of three branches, it is still run by people. No morals here. Don't care how they look at you. Winning is what matters.

That big win made all New York media notice Roy, and notice Donald. Roy thought it a chance: tell the cameras the Commodore dream. No need to ask father. Succeed, and father will take you. From then Roy was a firm friend and mentor, and Donald opened the map of the business empire that would later roar.
