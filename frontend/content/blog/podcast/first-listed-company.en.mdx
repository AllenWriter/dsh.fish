Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. After National Day, the topic on top is probably stocks.

One big green candle changes faith. Plenty of people spent the holiday waiting to run in. Reality is bony. Investing has risk. A few days of investor education, bone-deep, for the new crowd. Enter carefully. Today's box is this slaughterhouse — no, the story of stocks.

How did the thing so many people dream about get invented?

The world's first listed company debuts at the peak. Average yearly dividend 18%, at the high 40%, and it can keep paying like that for 200 years, flattening any glorious P2P era. Strip inflation and the market cap is a wild 7.9 trillion dollars, more than double today's three-trillion Apple.

What business prints that? And lists, and pays everyone? Is heaven that kind? Can I buy in now?

Don't rush. Open the report. Main business, in bold: colonial trade.

That's the first multinational, the first joint-stock limited company, founded 1602: the Dutch East India Company. When our national hero Zheng Chenggong drives out the Dutch colonizers, that's VOC.

The story starts with the great voyages. The reason is simple. The Ottoman Empire blasts Constantinople's gate with giant cannon. Eastern Rome falls. Constantinople becomes Istanbul.

If you've been to Turkey, or open a map, that spot is excellent: Asia-Europe join, close to the Mediterranean. Porcelain and spice from the east hit the land Silk Road, then ship in bulk to Europe.

Now that road is occupied. Venetian merchants jack the price. Even a thick royal purse can't take it. Send troops at the Ottomans? A dream. Europe isn't even of one mind. But eastern spice makes food sing, so pay the extra, pinch elsewhere.

This can't last. Whoever finds a way to smash the price, the court will fund. Portugal goes first on venture capital for sailors. Dias and da Gama find the route around the Cape to India. Turkey's monopoly breaks. They've found the upstream factory. No middleman. They can even sell the information gap to other European states.

Spain won't sit. They fund Columbus and find the Americas. Along with that, the state-secret hydrology, weather, and a stable route, all on the charts. Other countries know you can go to Asia via the Cape. They don't know the coastal water, or the Indian Ocean monsoon that lets ships run far past the Cape.

Build your own ships, buy the experience with money — not easy. You might get raided. Cheaper to ship on Portuguese middleman boats. Portugal monopolizes the eastern route for a hundred-plus years.

Latecomers have one chance to cut the corner. Guess? Steal. Commercial spies. Enter the Dutch merchants' hero, Jan Huyghen van Linschoten. We'll call him Old Yang.

Born in Holland, family to Portugal, joins the church, gets a job in Portuguese India. Spain and Portugal have a gentleman's deal: Portugal monopolizes east to India, Spain the Americas. Spain still wants a bite of the east, so they soak spies through the church.

Old Yang has his own abacus. He becomes a Spanish spy, collecting climate, water, customs along the India route. That's not extra enough. He privately collects from East Africa to the Arabian Sea, even Malacca. The work ethic moves the Portuguese. Promotions. Spain is delighted too.

After 14 years of spying, in 1595 he takes a pile of papers back to Europe — and picks a path nobody planned: build the motherland, Holland.

That same year Holland's first ocean fleet leaves, Old Yang's guide in hand. Four ships, 247 men, 100 guns, around Portuguese and pirate blockades, 15 months, Java — next door to today's Jakarta. Spain's monopoly, broken.

Once Portugal's eastern route is cracked, Dutch with open eyes keep sending fleets. Force and wealth grow. From 1595 to 1602, tiny Holland sprouts 14 trading companies. Average return per voyage 400%. Then it overcrowds. Everyone sails around the same time, everyone buys local goods, local prices explode. The swords and guns they brought land together. For the destination it becomes a buyer's market. Nice blades and muskets, take your pick.

The bosses look: this won't do. To avoid a race to the bottom, the 14 naturally think of a joint company to hold the price.

1602, the East India Company. Two years after the English one, but with financial innovation it later crushes EIC, even grows to ten times the size.

In the age of voyages, ships are expensive and the sea is dangerous. One slip, the hull flips. Dutch rich, however rich, have only just dipped into the soup. They cannot eat the whole loss alone.

Dutch people are hot for money. They've seen ships come home to auction at several times return. They want in.

A wild idea: can I make the company a thing you can trade? Split it down so anyone who wants in can buy. Spread the risk to the whole country. That's the stock. Ownership paper, traded. Investors who subscribe can take dividends on that paper.

1606, the first IPO in human history. VOC issues shares to the Dutch people on the Amsterdam exchange. Risk, spread nationwide. The fuse for later financial crises is also laid.

That's the cold knowledge for this episode. Next time: the soap around VOC — the first short, insider trades, the games of human nature that still shape the modern market.
