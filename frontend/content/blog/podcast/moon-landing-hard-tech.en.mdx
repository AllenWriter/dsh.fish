When you looked up as a child, did you also imagine a day when interstellar travel would be as easy as a city business trip, hopping around like a sci-fi film.

Grown up, we are busy with school and jobs. Even finding a place where you can clearly see the Milky Way is hard.

This Blind Box looks back at the sky, two episodes: the fun behind humanity's first moon trip, and the Mars plan still ahead.

We point at plans that look impossible, see what solutions scientists and experts used on a technical problem older than any of us. From a god's-eye view, what thinking is hiding behind those wild ideas. Feel the courage and hymn of the species.

People who pick stars must wear ten thousand zhang of light.

Almost half a century since the first landing. On July 21, 1968 — wait, the famous live day — more than 650 million people worldwide watched on TV. Almost every set was on NASA's channel. Crime even dipped. From go-ahead to a crewed landing: only seven years.

What? This is not how. This is on what grounds. On President Kennedy telling Congress in 1961: moon in ten years?

Nobody was more shocked than NASA scientists and engineers. Invent a moon ship in under a decade.

Nobody knew how to fly to the moon. How big a rocket. How small and powerful a computer. What the surface was like. What astronauts should eat.

A few despairing items and you see how hard.

The whole trip needs a computer, right? Tight math, right?

Astronauts need clothes. Radiation, cosmic particles, warm and cold. Not enough: that full-armor suit still has to bounce on the moon without cracking.

After you send people, at least don't lose them. Best, a live broadcast. Even now, a constant satellite link is not 100%.

Yes: the 1960s. Twenty years after the first computer. Most machines still ate half a room, did simple math. Personal microcomputers wait until 1971.

A big box, and the important memory is a few KB. Pack those and fire them to space? Don't even think.

The internet itself waits until after the landing. Near-zero-latency light transmission waits until the 80s. That is only the helper tech. Direct astronaut kit — even the suit's design and protection — was a giant materials challenge.

Landing itself had too many out-of-era difficulties. Americans said it themselves: like crossing the Pacific in a washing machine. (A modern washer's chip may well out-compute the lunar module.) Even if you time-traveled, the most optimistic person would struggle to believe it in that window.

So some people still think the U.S. hired Kubrick to shoot it on a soundstage.

Who most wants the landing to be fake? The old Soviet Union. Even the KGB could not disprove it. Astronauts left a laser ranging reflector and a moonquake meter. Still think it was fake?

So how did engineers and scientists beat the era? The answer is maybe surprising. Yes: old methods. No advanced tech, then human-wave tactics. No AI, then intelligence by hand.

Take the core moon computer. Apollo had two. One took the command module from Earth to Moon and back. One computed the lander's round trip between lunar orbit and the surface.

MIT's geniuses made the era's smallest, fastest, most flexible computer. Versus half-a-room machines, Apollo's was a briefcase, helping astronauts navigate.

Earth computers then needed punch cards, results in hours. Like tapping a calculate button on your phone and waiting hours.

Apollo's computer had a keyboard and answered at once. Unique design: core-rope memory, the densest mini-computer memory of the time. Experienced textile women had to thread wire to exact positions with a needle. Every strand perfect. Like knitting: wire through a core is 1, around a core is 0.

Only absolute precision keeps the program running. Behind Apollo, many great, ordinary, ignored women.

If you read The Three-Body Problem, the human-array computer and this "woven memory" share a principle. Both can be called semiconductors. A chip is processing countless 0s and 1s, binary.

Weaving computers is dull. It needs skill, experience, focus.

The factory women once struck briefly. Managers sat down to weave to keep schedule. They made scrap.

One hour of spaceflight, a million hours of effort on Earth.

Besides computers, mill workers wove the return-capsule parachutes.

The cloth had to be strong enough for a brutal descent, and light. A wisp of extra weight could push the lander over, miss the return orbit.

Each chute was pieced cloth, two million needle holes, stepped out on old sewing machines. One wrong stitch could be disaster.

Then workers folded them. During Apollo in the 1960s–70s, only three people in the whole U.S. were certified to fold Apollo chutes.

They were so important NASA forbade them to ride in the same car, in case a crash killed them together. They could not afford that accident.

Besides computers and chutes, NASA sent three electric cars to the moon. No oxygen in space, a gas car's engine would just die. When will the first NIO leap into outer space?

There is much more hard tech around the landing. The constant is human courage facing difficulty. Even a plan that looks impossible, with planning and collaboration, can bring unexpected science. Without Apollo, many modern conveniences would have waited.
