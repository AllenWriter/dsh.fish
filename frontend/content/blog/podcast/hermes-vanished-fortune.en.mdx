Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. Today: swallowing Hermès. An heir's vanished fortune, and a war inside a luxury empire.

Part one. Old Pi's disappearing wealth.

The story starts with an absurd question.

If you had ten billion euros, would you open the account every day and look?

A normal person would look more than once. Once before bed, once after waking up.

A Hermès heir took the other extreme.

He owned six million Hermès shares, worth about ten billion euros, and in his world those shares vanished for more than ten years. He found out very late.

His name is Nicolas Puech.

Too long. We'll call him Old Pi.

He is not an ordinary rich man. He is one of the fifth-generation Hermès heirs. The family started in saddlery, then leather, scarves, bags, a French workshop that grew into one of the hardest luxury brands on earth.

So when Old Pi was born, the ticket in his hand was not a normal entry pass. It was almost printed "top European family asset."

As a young man he went into Hermès, even as an executive.

Then he left.

He does not seem like the type who has to sit on the board and control everything. He ran his own fashion house, coached water skiing, then simply moved to Switzerland and lived the pastoral-billionaire life on Hermès dividends, somewhere in the European countryside.

Picture it.

The Alps, an estate, an old house, far from family meetings at Paris HQ, far from the daily knives of luxury.

For most people that's the ending.

For Old Pi it's where the disaster starts.

Because he did not watch the money.

Almost everything to do with property, he handed to one person.

Éric Freymond.

We'll call him Old Fu.

Old Fu is Geneva finance. Marriage into a local grand family, then a private-bank introduction to Old Pi. They've known each other for years. How deep?

In Old Pi's eyes, Old Fu is not a normal advisor.

He is friend, butler, secretariat, the person who handles the outside world.

He opens mail, takes calls, signs checks, even coaches Old Pi on how to talk to the Hermès family.

Note this. First danger signal.

A billionaire outsources his entire wealth system to one man.

Like a company handing finance rights, contract rights, email rights, board-communication rights, all to an outside consultant.

If the consultant is solid, that's peace of mind.

If not, that's giving the vault key to a locksmith.

Old Fu's real talent is not just money. He owns the gates.

The gate between Old Pi and the bank.

The gate between Old Pi and the lawyers.

The gate between Old Pi and the Hermès family.

Slowly Old Pi's wealth world sits under thick glass. Outside can't see in. Inside can only look out through Old Fu's one window.

Then two key moves.

First: blank signatures.

Old Pi signs blank papers in advance. Old Fu fills in numbers, words, deals, and acts in Old Pi's name.

Folks, that is not ordinary authorization.

That's handing someone a stack of contracts already chopped with the company seal, and trusting they'll only use it for the property fee.

Second: bearer shares.

Old Fu talks Old Pi into converting registered shares — easier to track — into bearer shares.

Registered shares are a house title with your name. Who holds, who transfers, when it changes: you can look.

Bearer shares are a vault key with no name. Whoever holds it has a lot of room to move.

You can say, in a Swiss nest of rich people, bearer shares have privacy and tax convenience.

They also have a fatal problem.

Once shares go bearer, the flow of assets walks from daylight into fog. Tracking where they went gets suddenly hard.

So the conditions for a crime are basically complete.

Old Pi trusts Old Fu completely.

Old Fu holds huge authority.

The stock is harder to follow.

Old Pi himself does not seriously check the books for years.

That is not ordinary carelessness.

That is a vacuum of control.

On paper the wealth is yours. In operation, it has started to stop listening.

What wakes Old Pi is not a bank, not a lawyer, not Hermès HQ.

It is the gardener's wife.

During the pandemic Old Pi gets very close to the Moroccan gardener's family on the estate. Gardener, wife, kids — he treats them as family.

He even plans to leave them a large piece of the fortune.

That already sounds like a dynasty movie.

A top European luxury heir, far from the old family system, growing intimate with the estate gardener, even rewriting the will.

The real blast is not in the will. It is in a transfer.

Late 2022, Old Pi asks Old Fu: I told you to send the gardener's family a million Swiss francs. How did that go?

Old Fu stammers.

After he leaves, the gardener's wife tells Old Pi: we never received that money.

A lot of big cases are not cracked by genius detectives. They are punched through by the simplest question.

Where's the money?

Old Pi starts to feel wrong.

He hires lawyers, consultants, a full check of his finances.

He looks, and his brain blanks.

The accounts have been empty for a long time.

Ninety percent of the Hermès shares were sold as early as 2008.

Meaning: while Old Pi still thought he was Hermès's largest individual shareholder, the floor under his wealth may already have been dug out.

Like living in a mansion, looking at crystal lights, oil paintings, the fireplace, thinking the house is still yours.

The title was transferred years ago.

Old Pi fires Old Fu in a rage and sues him in Geneva: decades of friendship, used to sell a fortune of Hermès stock behind his back.

The Geneva court, at first, does not stand with Old Pi.

The court's logic is cold.

You were too indifferent to your own finances. In front of Old Fu you almost fully gave up control. The evidence is not clear enough. Hard for a court to step in.

That sounds like legal language. In human: your money is gone, maybe someone cheated you.

But you were cheated this badly because you opened the door.

Old Pi does not quit.

In later years he files civil and criminal cases in France. Old Fu at first denies again and again: I never touched his shares.

In later investigation, Old Fu finally admits the shares were sold more than ten years ago.

He says Old Pi knew.

Old Pi of course says no.

The judge does not fully believe Old Fu either. Old Pi produces papers where Old Fu had promised the fortune was intact.

So Old Fu is preliminarily charged: forgery, using forged documents, aggravated abuse of trust.

Bank accounts frozen.

Art frozen.

Sculpture, furniture, frozen.

Then the story turns into a noir film.

July 2025, not long after a judicial interview, Old Fu is hit by a train near a holiday chalet in the Swiss Alps.

Police call it suicide.

The key person is dead.

What he takes with him is not only his life. It is a lot of detail in this fortune mystery that may never be clear.

So where did Old Pi's stock actually go?

If Old Fu alone was stuffing his pockets, the story is already strange enough.
