Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box.

Not long ago I flew to Singapore.

A few hours in the air, the fare wasn't cheap. After landing I looked at the ticket and a question popped: on this trip, how much did Singapore Airlines actually make?

Very likely: nothing.

For the quarter ending June this year, SIA Group revenue hit a record 5.7 billion Singapore dollars. More people flying, more tickets sold, and a net loss of 76 million.

One reason: fuel jumped hard in a single quarter. Sell tickets as well as you like — if oil, labor, and airport fees climb together, profit can vanish in a second.

That's the awkward bit of aviation. The ticket is the most visible product, and not necessarily the most reliable business.

So what do airlines live on?

I looked around. The things that are actually worth money are mainly three.

First: slots.

Not the time of day. A license: are you allowed to land at this airport at this hour, and take off again.

Hot airports have finite runways and stands. The gold morning and evening hours are one radish, one hole. Heathrow is the textbook. Capacity sits near full. A latecomer who wants one more golden-hour flight usually cannot just buy another plane. They have to get a slot first.

More important: slots come with "grandfather rights." Keep using a slot at the required rate, and next season you usually keep it. First in not only takes the seat, they renew it.

So a pair of gold slots can be insanely expensive.

In 2016 Oman Air reportedly paid 75 million dollars for a pair of Heathrow slots to add a morning flight. Before that, American Airlines bought a pair from SAS for 60 million.

They weren't buying a patch of concrete. They were buying entry to the world's most valuable aviation window.

The same plane at dawn and at midnight is not the same asset. Whether business travelers sit, whether a connection works, whether the aircraft can fly one more rotation, all hang on the slot.

So the first truly scarce airline asset is not the plane. It's the position. Planes you can buy. Golden slots, someone may not sell.

Position alone is not enough.

Second asset: the network.

Aviation has a special rule. The more cities, the faster the number of possible itineraries grows.

Three points, at most six directed links. Ten points, ninety. Add one city and you don't just add one route. You may connect to every other city on the net.

That's a hub's value.

Fly people into one hub, then out to different destinations. Two thin routes, snapped together at a hub, can fill a plane.

By that logic SIA and Emirates shouldn't have an edge.

Singapore is a city-state, almost no domestic market. After SIA went independent in 1972, almost everything was international. Dubai's local population is limited too, no hinterland big enough for a huge network.

They hit on the same trick. Not enough locals? Bring the world's connecting traffic to your door.

The industry calls it "sixth freedom."

The name sounds fancy. The idea is simple. London to Sydney doesn't have to be BA or Qantas nonstop. Emirates to Dubai, then Dubai to Sydney.

Emirates stitches "UK to UAE" and "UAE to Australia" into a "London to Sydney" product.

SIA does the same at Changi. They don't sell "where Singaporeans want to go." They sell "where the world can go via Singapore."

About 70% of Emirates passengers are only connecting in Dubai. Rough booking numbers: maybe 30 million transfer passengers a year, 100,000 a day walking through Dubai.

They don't lack a hinterland. They borrow everyone else's and weave it into their net.

Fun part: the net started tiny.

In 1985 Dubai decides to found an airline. Seed money 10 million dollars, five months to prepare, the first two aircraft leased from PIA.

October 25 that year, Emirates' first flight, Dubai to Karachi.

Forty years later, 150-plus cities, last fiscal year about 5.4 billion dollars of profit, a record. Not in Star, oneworld, or SkyTeam. From Dubai they wove their own global net.

SIA takes another road.

Changi becomes a transfer factory: SIA for the high end, Scoot for low cost, Star Alliance to pull other airlines' passengers into the net.

No domestic market is a weakness. Because there's no way back, connecting becomes the main job.

At this point I thought the logic was clear: hold scarce slots, weave a big enough net around a hub.

Then there's a third thing, even less like an airline business.

Miles.

We treat miles as a gift: fly, bank points, cash them for a ticket or an upgrade.

The real miles business is not giving points to passengers. It is selling points to banks.

Banks buy airline miles, wrap them as credit-card rewards. Every swipe of a co-branded card, the bank pays the airline, the airline credits your account.

Two gifts for the carrier.

One: the passenger may redeem in six months or years. The bank's money is already in.

Two: some miles never get used. Even when they do, the airline often gives a seat that might have flown empty. Cost is not the ticket price.

Delta SkyMiles, 2019: about 6.1 billion dollars cash from selling miles. Over three-quarters from third parties. Biggest buyer, American Express, about 4 billion that year.

United even mortgaged MileagePlus and raised 5 billion.

Miles are no longer just points. They're a financial asset you can sell, finance, and pull cash on before anyone flies.

SIA's KrisFlyer is the same.

Started 1999. By March 2025, members past 10 million, more than 1,700 partner brands where you can earn or burn on the ground, the most important class being banks and card issuers.

You think you're swiping toward a free ticket. Each swipe, the bank may already have paid the airline for you.

So: cheaper tickets, the airline looks more like a transport company. Stronger miles plan, it looks more like a finance company.

Back to the first question. If tickets don't make money, what do airlines live on?

Three sentences.

Hold the gold slots, so you have the right to appear in the most valuable places.

Weave a net around a hub, and turn the world's passing traffic into your passengers.

Sell miles to banks, and take the cash back before anyone sits down.

Of course an airline cannot live with zero ticket revenue. That ticket is just not the whole thing. It's more like an entry pass: into the network, onto a connection, into miles, onto a credit card, keeping the machine running.

Later I looked at that Singapore ticket again.

The money I paid was, of course, for a flight. What holds SIA's long-term edge is not how much this one plane made. It is whether they can keep slots, grow the net, and wire 10 million members to 1,700 brands.

SIA and Emirates prove the same thing:

What aviation is truly short of has never been only the seat.

It is whether you can hold the position, weave the net, and turn one flight into a business that happens again and again.

Not enough people at the door is fine.

As long as enough people pass through.
