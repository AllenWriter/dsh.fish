Hello everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box.

How are your stocks lately? Today another bubble-burst story: the U.S. railroad bubble of 1873.

If you only look at the ending, it looks like a pack of con men cutting leeks with new tech. Problem: the railroad was not air. It really linked America, really made goods faster, grew cities, really changed the whole country.

If the tech is real and demand is real, why does it still become a bubble?

That question may be more worth talking than "why bubbles pop."

One

May 10, 1869, Utah Territory, Promontory Summit.

Two construction crews finally met on the waste. Union Pacific from the east, Central Pacific from the west, joined here. A golden spike on the site, telegraph lines to the whole country, the last hammer, news almost at once in every big city.

The whole country boiled.

Before, east coast to west, wagon, horse, around South America, or gamble the waste, months. Now a transcontinental railroad squeezed the trip to about a week.

Friends, this is not a PPT, not a concept video. A tech revolution you can see with the eye.

Railroad was not only speed. Midwest wheat to New York, eastern machines inland, mines, ranches, ports, factories on one net. Before, whether a place had business depended on how close the river was. After, whether a place could live depended on whether the train stopped.

So people then who believed in railroads were not stupid.

The real trouble: they quickly swapped a true sentence — "railroads will change America" — for another — "any railroad will make money."

Those two look a few words apart. Between them sits a graveyard.

Two

To build a transcontinental, the biggest problem is not whether you have a dream. Who pays.

Railroads are expensive. Steel, ties, locomotives, blast mountains, bridges, thousands of workers. Worse: before it is through, almost no income; after it is through, whether there are enough people and goods along the line, nobody knows.

Private capital would not bet alone, so the U.S. government stepped in.

1862, Lincoln signed the Pacific Railway Act. Government offered bond support, and handed huge public land along the line to the companies. Some project incentives can even be read as: every mile you lay, besides financing, you get sheets of land on both sides.

The design had a reason. Waste land then was cheap. Railroad through, land can sell. Government need not put out all cash at once, and can still lure private companies to finish a national project.

It also made a very dangerous temptation.

How should a railroad company earn? Of course move people and goods from A to B, tickets and freight.

Now, before it has hauled a few cars, it can first get land, bonds, and contracts by building. So the operators' main question slowly goes from "does this line have passengers" to "can this line lay another fifty miles."

Railroad went from a transport business to a mileage race.

More brilliant operations later.

A batch of insiders at Union Pacific controlled a construction contractor named Crédit Mobilier. Long name. Just remember: it is the railroad company's shadow clone.

Left hand the railroad, raising from government and investors. Right hand the builder, quoting and laying. The masters of both hands, often the same people.

The result has imagination.

Right hand inflates cost, left hand pays in full. The railroad's books carry more and more cost, the builder's shareholders can pocket profit early. Whether trains earn later is a future problem. Whether the contractor earns today is now.

Later Congress found the company not only overcharged the railroad and government, it also handed profitable shares to some congressmen, trying to make Washington ask fewer questions.

Look: the railroad had not yet delivered the nation, and had already delivered some people's wealth.

Three

But government subsidy and insider deals alone cannot blow a national bubble. What really spread railroad fever was bonds.

Builders need money today, promise to repay with decades of future freight. Investors buy railroad bonds, betting one thing: wherever the rails go, people, farms, mines, cities will follow. As long as America keeps expanding west, today's line through wilderness becomes tomorrow's gold channel.

The story is very charming, because it explains "no demand yet" as "demand is on the way."

The person best at selling that story was Jay Cooke. We'll call him Old Ku.

Old Ku was not an ordinary banker. In the Civil War he helped the Union sell war bonds to the public. Fame, channels, credit, all top. Simply: others sell bonds on interest. Old Ku sells on faith.

After the war he eyed a grander project: the Northern Pacific.

The line would go from the Great Lakes, through northern prairie and the Rockies, to the Pacific coast. On the map it looks like a zipper pulled sideways, sewing the whole north together.

Problem: a line on a map is thin. A railroad in reality is expensive.

The route crosses a lot of thin-population country. No ready big cities for passengers, not enough freight to pay debt at once. It must burn money to build, then wait for immigrants, farms, commerce to appear slowly.

In other words Northern Pacific was not selling a railroad that already earned. It was selling a world that would be born because of the railroad.

1870, work officially started. Old Ku's bank underwrote the bonds, sold them through a huge net to U.S. and European investors. Every stretch the railroad extended, the next batch of investors had to keep paying.

As long as bonds sold, work could continue. As long as work continued, people believed bonds would sell.

A perfect loop.

Until it stopped looping.

Four

1873, European finance broke first.

May, Vienna stock market crashed. By September European investors started dumping U.S. railroad securities, pulling money back. Capital that had been flowing to U.S. rails suddenly went out with the tide.

Northern Pacific had by then reached today's North Dakota, costs overrunning, line income far from enough. What it lacked most was not rails. It was the next person willing to take the bonds.

September 18, 1873, Old Ku's bank announced it would stop payments.

The shock to Americans then is about waking up, finding the country's best Treasury-bond seller, the most trusted finance star, saying in the afternoon: sorry, the money does not connect.

Panic spread at once.

Investors rushed banks and brokers, banks rushed railroad companies for debt. Railroads had been borrowing new money to build old work. Once financing stopped, rails half-laid, wages unpaid, equipment sellers unpaid. Not one company falling. A whole chain of people owing each other.

September 20, the New York Stock Exchange had to halt trading. First time in its history it closed the doors for a financial panic.

Many railroad projects stopped, banks and brokerages failed, workers unemployed. The crisis later called the Panic of 1873.

So when the U.S. railroad bubble really burst, it was not one day everyone suddenly found trains useless.

The opposite. Trains were very useful.

What collapsed was something else: the unified imagination that every line is worth building, every debt can be repaid, every waste will become a city on schedule — that story of the future.

Five

After the railroad bubble, did America stop building railroads?

Of course not.

Northern Pacific later changed hands, work went on, finally did reach the Pacific. The U.S. rail net kept expanding after, and really shaped a national market.

That is the most interesting part of this story.

If a scam bursts, usually nothing is left. After an infrastructure bubble bursts, the rails are still there, stations, bridges, tunnels. Later people can even take, cheap, assets the last investors built with heavy money.

Failed companies may have paved the successful age.

That does not comfort the investors of the day. "Society finally benefits" and "you can make money" were never the same thing.

A railroad may be very important to the country, and still, because cost is too high, routes duplicate, demand comes too late, debt too heavy, leave the first shareholders and creditors with nothing.

A technology may unquestionably change the world, and still not guarantee every company flying its flag has profit.

The biggest railroad-bubble mistake: people took tech progress, company profit, and asset prices as the same thing.

They are three things.

Tech progress answers: is this useful?

Company profit answers: who can keep earning with it?

Asset prices answer: how much did we pay today for this future?

The first two can both be "yes." The third can still be "too expensive."

So today, when we see AI, chips, data centers, or any tech called "the new railroad," the real question may not be whether it will change the world.

It probably will.

The harder question: who is laying the road, who is selling shovels, who is collecting tolls, who can only live on continuous financing? And when everyone believes the future will arrive, have we already prepaid decades of good news into today's price?

Americans in 1873 did not misread the railroad.

They only imagined a long, winding future that needs constant culling as a ticket you can cash at once.

The railroad was real.

The bubble was real.

History's most expensive lessons often hide where those two sentences are both true.
